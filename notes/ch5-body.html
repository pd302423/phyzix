CBSE Class 11 Physics · Chapter 5Work, Energy and Power

Complete chapter notes — concepts, every derivation worked in full, standard results, worked examples and the traps that cost marks.

The spine of this chapter: work is the transfer of energy, and everything else is bookkeeping. $W_{\text{net}} = \Delta K$ holds always, with no exceptions. Add potential energy to the ledger and you get conservation of mechanical energy — but only when the forces doing work are conservative.

1 · Work

1.1 Definition

$W = \vec F\cdot\vec d = Fd\cos\theta$  — a scalar. Unit: joule (J) $=$ N m.  $\theta$ is the angle between the force and the displacement.

Work is done only when a force produces a displacement and has a component along it. Holding a heavy bag stationary does no work in the physics sense, however tired you get.

1.2 Sign of work

AngleSignStandard examples
$\theta < 90^\circ$PositiveApplied force on a moving block; gravity on a falling body
$\theta = 90^\circ$ZeroCentripetal force; normal reaction on a horizontal floor; tension on a bob in circular motion; the force on a body carried horizontally
$\theta > 90^\circ$NegativeFriction on a sliding block; gravity on a body moving upward
Frequently asked
"Why is no work done by the centripetal force?" Because it acts perpendicular to the instantaneous displacement at every point, so $W = Fs\cos90^\circ = 0$. The force changes the direction of the velocity, never its magnitude — and it is only the magnitude that carries kinetic energy.

1.3 Work done by a variable force

When $F$ changes with position, split the displacement into pieces so small that $F$ is effectively constant across each one. Over a piece $dx$ the work is $dW = F\,dx$, and the total is the sum:

$W = \displaystyle\int_{x_1}^{x_2} F\,dx$  = the area under the $F$–$x$ graph between $x_1$ and $x_2$. Area below the axis counts as negative work.

2 · Kinetic energy

$K = \tfrac12 mv^2 = \dfrac{p^2}{2m}$  and  $p = \sqrt{2mK}$  — a scalar, always positive, measured in joules.

2.1 A pattern worth spotting

Three results in your syllabus share one algebraic shape:

ResultWhere it comes from
$W = \dfrac{F^2}{2k}$Work done stretching a spring by a given force $F$
$K = \dfrac{p^2}{2m}$Linear kinetic energy in terms of momentum
$K_{\text{rot}} = \dfrac{L^2}{2I}$Rotational kinetic energy in terms of angular momentum (Ch 6)

All three have the form (quantity)² ÷ (2 × inertia-like term). In every case, if the numerator is held fixed, the answer is inversely proportional to the denominator.

Exam application
Two springs stretched by the same force, with $k_A > k_B$: since $W \propto 1/k$, we get $W_A < W_B$ — more work is done on the softer spring B. Two bodies with equal angular momenta, $I_A > I_B$: since $K \propto 1/I$, we get $K_A < K_B$ — body B has the greater kinetic energy. These are the same question in two costumes.

Also worth carrying: for equal momenta, the lighter body has more KE; for equal KE, the heavier body has more momentum.

3 · The Work–Energy Theorem

Statement. The work done by the net force acting on a body equals the change in its kinetic energy.  $W_{\text{net}} = \Delta K = \tfrac12mv^2 - \tfrac12mu^2$
DERIVATION 1  Work–energy theorem — constant force, then variable force

Part A — constant force (1-D).

  1. Start from the third equation of motion: $v^2 = u^2 + 2as$.
  2. Multiply throughout by $\tfrac12 m$:  $\tfrac12mv^2 - \tfrac12mu^2 = mas$.
  3. But $ma = F$ and $Fs = W$, so the right-hand side is exactly the work done.
  4. $\Rightarrow W = \tfrac12mv^2 - \tfrac12mu^2 = \Delta K$.

Part B — variable force. This is the version usually asked, because it needs calculus.

  1. For a small displacement $dx$ the work done is $dW = F\,dx$, so the total work is $W = \displaystyle\int_{x_1}^{x_2}F\,dx$.
  2. By Newton's second law $F = ma = m\dfrac{dv}{dt}$.
  3. Use the chain rule to change the variable: $\dfrac{dv}{dt} = \dfrac{dv}{dx}\cdot\dfrac{dx}{dt} = v\dfrac{dv}{dx}$.
  4. Substitute: $dW = \left(mv\dfrac{dv}{dx}\right)dx = mv\,dv$.
  5. Integrate between the initial and final speeds: $W = \displaystyle\int_{u}^{v} mv\,dv = m\left[\dfrac{v^2}{2}\right]_{u}^{v}$.
  6. $\Rightarrow W = \tfrac12mv^2 - \tfrac12mu^2 = K_f - K_i$.
$W_{\text{net}} = \Delta K$. Positive net work ⟹ the body speeds up; negative ⟹ it slows down; zero ⟹ the speed is unchanged.
Where this loses marks
  1. The theorem uses the net work of all forces — gravity and friction included — not just the applied force.
  2. If the question says "for a variable force", you must show the $\int F\,dx = \int mv\,dv$ route. The $v^2 = u^2+2as$ argument alone will not earn full marks.

4 · Potential energy

Potential energy is energy stored by virtue of position or configuration. It is defined only for conservative forces.

4.1 Gravitational PE near the Earth's surface

$U = mgh$, taking $g$ as constant over the height involved. Only changes in $U$ are physically meaningful, so you may set the zero level wherever it is convenient.

4.2 Elastic potential energy of a spring

DERIVATION 2  Potential energy of a spring, $U = \tfrac12kx^2$
  1. Hooke's law. When a spring is stretched or compressed by $x$ from its natural length, it exerts a restoring force $F_s = -kx$. The minus sign says the force opposes the displacement; $k$ is the spring constant, in N m−1.
  2. To stretch the spring slowly (no kinetic energy gained), an external agent must apply an equal and opposite force $F_{\text{ext}} = +kx$ at every instant.
  3. Work done by the agent over a further small stretch $dx$:  $dW = kx\,dx$.
  4. Total work from the natural length to extension $x$:  $W = \displaystyle\int_0^{x} kx\,dx = k\left[\dfrac{x^2}{2}\right]_0^{x} = \tfrac12kx^2$.
  5. This work is stored in the spring as elastic potential energy: $U(x) = \tfrac12kx^2$. (The spring itself does $-\tfrac12kx^2$ of work.)
  6. Geometrically it is the area of the triangle under the $F$–$x$ line: $\tfrac12 \times x \times kx$.
$U(x) = \tfrac12kx^2$. Work done in going from $x_1$ to $x_2$ is $\tfrac12k(x_2^2 - x_1^2)$ — not $\tfrac12k(x_2-x_1)^2$.

4.3 Conservative and non-conservative forces

For a conservative force in one dimension,

$F = -\dfrac{dU}{dx}$  — the force is the negative slope of the PE curve. Where $U$ is a minimum the equilibrium is stable; where it is a maximum, unstable.

5 · Conservation of mechanical energy

$E = K + U = \text{constant}$, provided the forces doing work are conservative. With friction present: $\Delta K + \Delta U = W_{\text{non-conservative}}$.
DERIVATION 3  Conservation of mechanical energy for a freely falling body

A body of mass $m$ is released from rest at height $H$. Take the ground as the reference level ($U = 0$) and ignore air resistance, so gravity is the only force doing work.

  1. At the top. $v = 0$, so $K = 0$; $U = mgH$.  Total $E = 0 + mgH = mgH$.
  2. At a depth $x$ below the top (height $H - x$). Using $v^2 = u^2 + 2gx$ with $u = 0$: $v^2 = 2gx$.
    $K = \tfrac12m(2gx) = mgx$  and  $U = mg(H-x)$.
    Total $E = mgx + mgH - mgx = mgH$.
  3. At the ground. $v^2 = 2gH$, so $K = \tfrac12m(2gH) = mgH$; $U = 0$.  Total $E = mgH$.
  4. The total is the same at all three points, so mechanical energy is conserved. Potential energy is steadily converted into kinetic energy.
$E = mgH$ throughout. Halfway down ($x = H/2$): $K = U = mgH/2$. The speed on reaching the ground is $\sqrt{2gH}$ — independent of the mass.

6 · Power

$P_{\text{avg}} = \dfrac{W}{t}$  ·  $P_{\text{inst}} = \dfrac{dW}{dt} = \vec F\cdot\vec v = Fv\cos\theta$  — unit watt (W) $=$ J s−1.
  • $1\ \text{horsepower} = 746\ \text{W}$
  • $1\ \text{kWh} = 3.6\times10^{6}\ \text{J}$ — this is a unit of energy, not power. The "per hour" is part of the unit, not a rate.

7 · Collisions

Momentum is conserved in every collision. Kinetic energy is conserved only in an elastic collision. Write this sentence in any collision answer — it is often a mark on its own.

During a collision the internal forces are enormous and the contact time tiny, so external forces (gravity, friction) contribute negligible impulse. That is why momentum conservation holds even on a rough floor.

7.1 Elastic collision in one dimension

DERIVATION 4  Elastic collision in 1-D — final velocities

Masses $m_1, m_2$ move along one line with initial velocities $u_1, u_2$ and final velocities $v_1, v_2$.

  1. Conservation of momentum: $m_1u_1 + m_2u_2 = m_1v_1 + m_2v_2$
    Group the like masses:  $m_1(u_1 - v_1) = m_2(v_2 - u_2)$  … (i)
  2. Conservation of kinetic energy: $\tfrac12m_1u_1^2 + \tfrac12m_2u_2^2 = \tfrac12m_1v_1^2 + \tfrac12m_2v_2^2$
    Cancel the $\tfrac12$ and group:  $m_1(u_1^2 - v_1^2) = m_2(v_2^2 - u_2^2)$
    Factorise both differences of squares:  $m_1(u_1-v_1)(u_1+v_1) = m_2(v_2-u_2)(v_2+u_2)$  … (ii)
  3. Divide (ii) by (i) — the masses and the bracketed differences cancel:
    $u_1 + v_1 = u_2 + v_2$  $\Rightarrow$  $u_1 - u_2 = v_2 - v_1$  … (iii)
    In words: relative velocity of approach = relative velocity of separation. This is the signature of an elastic collision.
  4. Solve (i) and (iii) as simultaneous equations in $v_1$ and $v_2$.
$v_1 = \left(\dfrac{m_1-m_2}{m_1+m_2}\right)u_1 + \left(\dfrac{2m_2}{m_1+m_2}\right)u_2$

$v_2 = \left(\dfrac{m_2-m_1}{m_1+m_2}\right)u_2 + \left(\dfrac{2m_1}{m_1+m_2}\right)u_1$

The three special cases (with $u_2 = 0$)

Case$v_1$$v_2$Physical picture
$m_1 = m_2$$0$$u_1$The velocities are exchanged — carrom, Newton's cradle. The first body stops dead, the second moves off with the first one's speed.
$m_1 \gg m_2$

$m_1 \ll m_2$

Frequently asked
"What happens when a light body collides elastically with a massive stationary body?" Substitute $m_1 \ll m_2$ and $u_2 = 0$ into the results: $v_1 \approx -u_1$ and $v_2 \approx 0$. The light body rebounds with its original speed reversed, and the massive body remains at rest. Its kinetic energy is essentially unchanged.

7.2 Perfectly inelastic collision

DERIVATION 5  Perfectly inelastic collision — common velocity and energy lost
  1. $m_1$ moving with $u_1$ strikes $m_2$ at rest, and the two stick together and move with a common velocity $v$.
  2. Momentum: $m_1u_1 = (m_1+m_2)v \Rightarrow v = \dfrac{m_1u_1}{m_1+m_2}$.
  3. Initial KE: $K_i = \tfrac12m_1u_1^2$.  Final KE: $K_f = \tfrac12(m_1+m_2)v^2 = \dfrac{m_1^2u_1^2}{2(m_1+m_2)}$.
  4. $\Delta K = K_i - K_f = \tfrac12m_1u_1^2\left[1 - \dfrac{m_1}{m_1+m_2}\right]$.
$v = \dfrac{m_1u_1}{m_1+m_2}$  ·  $\Delta K_{\text{lost}} = \dfrac{1}{2}\dfrac{m_1m_2}{m_1+m_2}u_1^2 > 0$  ·  fraction lost $= \dfrac{m_2}{m_1+m_2}$
The lost energy goes into heat, sound and permanent deformation. Momentum is still exactly conserved.

7.3 Coefficient of restitution

$e = \dfrac{\text{relative velocity of separation}}{\text{relative velocity of approach}} = \dfrac{|v_2-v_1|}{|u_1-u_2|}$ — dimensionless. $e=1$ perfectly elastic, $e=0$ perfectly inelastic, $0<e<1$ partially elastic.

A ball dropped from height $h$ rebounds to $h' = e^2h$; after $n$ bounces, $h_n = e^{2n}h$. So $e = \sqrt{h'/h}$.

7.4 Elastic collision in two dimensions

With $m_2$ initially at rest and the bodies flying off at angles $\theta_1$ and $\theta_2$:

  • $x$-momentum: $m_1u_1 = m_1v_1\cos\theta_1 + m_2v_2\cos\theta_2$
  • $y$-momentum: $0 = m_1v_1\sin\theta_1 - m_2v_2\sin\theta_2$
  • Energy: $\tfrac12m_1u_1^2 = \tfrac12m_1v_1^2 + \tfrac12m_2v_2^2$

Three equations, four unknowns — so one quantity must be supplied by the question.

DERIVATION 6  Equal masses in 2-D separate at 90°
  1. Equal masses, target at rest. Momentum (as vectors): $\vec u = \vec v_1 + \vec v_2$.
  2. Kinetic energy (the $\tfrac12 m$ cancels): $u^2 = v_1^2 + v_2^2$.
  3. Square the vector equation: $u^2 = (\vec v_1+\vec v_2)\cdot(\vec v_1+\vec v_2) = v_1^2 + v_2^2 + 2\,\vec v_1\cdot\vec v_2$.
  4. Compare with step 2: the extra term must vanish, so $\vec v_1\cdot\vec v_2 = 0$.
$\vec v_1 \perp \vec v_2$ — after an elastic collision between equal masses (one initially at rest), the two separate at 90°, provided both actually move.

8 · Motion in a vertical circle

A body of mass $m$ is tied to a string of length $R$ and whirled in a vertical circle. The speed is not constant — gravity does work as the height changes — so you need both a force equation and an energy equation.

DERIVATION 7  Vertical circle — minimum speeds and $T_{\text{bot}} - T_{\text{top}} = 6mg$
  1. At the highest point, both the tension $T_t$ and the weight $mg$ point downward — toward the centre. So they together supply the centripetal force:
    $T_t + mg = \dfrac{mv_t^2}{R}$
  2. A string can only pull, never push, so the critical condition is $T_t = 0$ (the string just goes slack):
    $mg = \dfrac{mv_t^2}{R} \Rightarrow v_t^{\min} = \sqrt{gR}$
  3. Energy conservation from the top to the bottom — a drop of $2R$, with only gravity doing work:
    $\tfrac12mv_b^2 = \tfrac12mv_t^2 + mg(2R) \Rightarrow v_b^2 = v_t^2 + 4gR$
  4. Put $v_t^2 = gR$ for the critical case: $v_b^2 = gR + 4gR = 5gR \Rightarrow v_b^{\min} = \sqrt{5gR}$
  5. At the lowest point, $T_b$ points up (toward the centre) and $mg$ points down:
    $T_b - mg = \dfrac{mv_b^2}{R}$
  6. Subtract the top equation from the bottom one:
    $T_b - T_t = \dfrac{m}{R}\left(v_b^2 - v_t^2\right) + 2mg = \dfrac{m}{R}(4gR) + 2mg$
$v_{\text{top}}^{\min} = \sqrt{gR}$  ·  $v_{\text{bottom}}^{\min} = \sqrt{5gR}$  ·  $v_{\text{side}}^{\min} = \sqrt{3gR}$
$T_{\text{bot}} - T_{\text{top}} = 6mg$ — and this holds at any speed, because $v_b^2 - v_t^2 = 4gR$ always follows from energy conservation.
At the critical speed: $T_{\text{top}}=0$, $T_{\text{bot}}=6mg$, $T_{\text{side}}=3mg$.

8.1 General position on the circle

Let $u$ be the speed at the lowest point and $\theta$ the angle turned from there, so the height gained is $h = R(1-\cos\theta)$:

$v = \sqrt{u^2 - 2gR(1-\cos\theta)}$  ·  $T = \dfrac{m}{R}\left[u^2 - 2gR(1-\cos\theta)\right] + mg\cos\theta$

Check the two ends: at $\theta = 0$ (bottom) this gives $T = \dfrac{mu^2}{R} + mg$; at $\theta = 180^\circ$ (top) it gives $T = \dfrac{mv_{\text{top}}^2}{R} - mg$. ✓

For a rod, or a bead inside a tube, the minimum speed at the top is zero — because a rod can push as well as pull, so it can support the body at the top with no speed at all.

Frequently asked
"A motorcyclist rides in a vertical circle. What is the necessary condition so that he does not fall?" His speed at the highest point must satisfy $v \ge \sqrt{rg}$, so that gravity is not more than what is needed to bend his path around the circle.
Traps that cost marks
  1. In $W = Fd\cos\theta$, $\theta$ is between the force and the displacement — not between the force and the horizontal.
  2. The work–energy theorem uses NET work of all forces. And if the question specifies a variable force, show the integral.
  3. Momentum is conserved in inelastic collisions too — only kinetic energy is lost. Never try to conserve KE in an inelastic problem.
  4. Spring work from $x_1$ to $x_2$ is $\tfrac12k(x_2^2-x_1^2)$, not $\tfrac12k(x_2-x_1)^2$.
  5. Minimum speed at the top of a vertical circle is $\sqrt{gR}$, not zero. Zero applies only to a rod or a tube.
  6. 1 kWh is energy, not power.
  7. Heat produced by friction is $f\times$(relative sliding distance), which is not the same as the work done by friction on one block when both bodies move.
  8. Equal masses in an elastic collision exchange velocities — they do not both come to rest. That is a favourite false assertion in assertion–reason questions.
