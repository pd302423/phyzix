CBSE Class 11 Physics · Chapter 4Laws of Motion

Complete chapter notes — concepts, every derivation worked in full, standard results, worked examples and the traps that cost marks.

The one idea that organises this chapter: force does not maintain motion — it changes motion. Every confusion in Chapter 4 comes from carrying Aristotle's intuition (things stop unless you keep pushing) into Newton's framework. Hold that sentence and the rest follows.

1 · Aristotle's fallacy and the law of inertia

1.1 What Aristotle got wrong

Aristotle claimed that an external force is required to keep a body moving with constant velocity. Push a book across a table; it slides and stops. Stop pushing and it stops. So — force sustains motion.

This is wrong, but it is worth understanding why it is seductive. It is not a stupid theory: it is an accurate description of what you actually observe. The error is that Aristotle never noticed that friction is a force too. The book stops not because the push "ran out", but because friction acted on it the whole time. On a frictionless surface the book would never stop.

Why this matters for you: whenever a body moves at constant velocity, your instinct will insist there must be a net force in the direction of motion. There is not. The net force is exactly zero.

1.2 Galileo's argument — the reason the first law is believable

Do not accept the first law as a bare assertion. Galileo argued for it, and the argument is examinable.

  1. Take two inclined planes facing each other. Release a ball down one; it rolls up the other and reaches almost the same height.
  2. Reduce the angle of the second plane. The ball still climbs to the same height — so it must travel a longer distance along the slope to get there.
  3. Reduce the angle further. Longer still.
  4. Now the limiting step: make the second plane horizontal. The ball can never reach its original height, so it must keep going forever.

Conclusion: in the absence of friction, a body in motion continues in motion with no force acting. That final step — pushing a trend to its limit — is the actual physics, and examiners like to see it.

2 · Newton's First Law

Statement. A body continues in its state of rest, or of uniform motion in a straight line, unless it is compelled by a net external force to change that state.

Three things the law genuinely says, which students routinely miss:

(a) "Net" external force

A body with four forces acting on it that sum to zero obeys the first law perfectly. Zero net force does not mean no forces. A book on a table has two forces on it and is in equilibrium.

(b) Rest and uniform motion are the same state

Physically there is no difference between sitting still and moving at 20 m s−1 in a straight line. Both are simply "zero acceleration". Which one you observe depends on the frame you are watching from.

(c) It defines the inertial frame

This is the deep content. The first law is not a special case of $F=ma$. It tells you in which frames $F=ma$ is allowed to be used at all — namely, frames in which a free body has zero acceleration. Those are inertial frames.

2.1 Inertial and non-inertial frames

An inertial frame is one in which Newton's first law holds. Any frame moving with constant velocity relative to an inertial frame is also inertial.

A non-inertial frame is one in which the first law fails — a body with no net force on it does not stay at rest or in uniform motion. Equivalently: any frame that is accelerating, whether linearly or by rotating.

The observable symptom is that you see accelerations with no force causing them. In a braking bus you lurch forward and nobody pushed you. On a merry-go-round you are flung outward with no agent pulling you.

Examples: an accelerating or braking vehicle; a lift speeding up or slowing down; any rotating frame. Strictly the Earth is non-inertial too (it spins and orbits), but the effect is so small that we treat it as inertial in every school problem.

$F = ma$ is valid only in inertial frames. That is exactly why lift problems are solved from the ground, never from inside the lift.

Exam note
This is almost always a 1-mark definition. Give the definition, one example, and the line "Newton's laws are valid only in inertial frames." Pseudo-forces are not required at Class 11 CBSE level — do not spend time on them.

2.2 Inertia and mass

Inertia is a body's resistance to a change in its state of motion. Mass is the quantitative measure of inertia. A heavier body is harder to start, harder to stop and harder to turn.

Type of inertiaMeaningStandard example
Inertia of restA body at rest resists being set in motionDust flies off when a carpet is beaten — the carpet moves, the dust stays
Inertia of motionA moving body resists being stoppedYou fall forward when a bus stops suddenly
Inertia of directionA body resists a change in its direction of motionMud flies off a spinning tyre along the tangent

3 · Momentum and Newton's Second Law

3.1 Why momentum is the right quantity

Ask what makes a moving object hard to stop. Not speed alone — a bullet at 500 m s−1 and a truck at 5 m s−1 are both dangerous. Not mass alone — a parked truck is harmless. The combination that matters is

$\vec p = m\vec v$  — linear momentum, a vector along the velocity. SI unit: kg m s−1.

The physical claim underneath the second law is this: the same force applied for the same time produces the same change in momentum, whatever the mass. That is why momentum, not velocity, is the natural variable. Newton called it "quantity of motion".

3.2 Newton's Second Law

Statement. The rate of change of momentum of a body is directly proportional to the applied force and takes place in the direction of the force.
DERIVATION 1  $\vec F = d\vec p/dt \Rightarrow \vec F = m\vec a$, and impulse
  1. Momentum is $\vec p = m\vec v$. The second law in its general form is $\vec F = \dfrac{d\vec p}{dt}$.
  2. Differentiate the product: $\dfrac{d\vec p}{dt} = \dfrac{d(m\vec v)}{dt} = m\dfrac{d\vec v}{dt} + \vec v\dfrac{dm}{dt}$.
  3. For a body of constant mass, $dm/dt = 0$, so the second term vanishes.
  4. $\Rightarrow \vec F = m\dfrac{d\vec v}{dt} = m\vec a$.
  5. Rearranging the general form gives $\vec F\,dt = d\vec p$. Integrating over the contact time from $t_1$ to $t_2$:  $\displaystyle\int_{t_1}^{t_2}\vec F\,dt = \vec p_2 - \vec p_1$.
$\vec F = m\vec a$ (constant mass)  ·  Impulse $\vec J = \vec F_{\text{avg}}\Delta t = \Delta\vec p$

Four things to internalise about $F = ma$:

  • It is a vector equation, so it splits into independent component equations: $F_x = ma_x$, $F_y = ma_y$. This is how every free-body-diagram problem actually gets solved.
  • It is instantaneous. The force right now determines the acceleration right now — not the average, not the history.
  • It defines the newton. 1 N is the force that gives a 1 kg mass an acceleration of 1 m s−2. So 1 N = 1 kg m s−2.
  • $F = ma$ is the special case; $F = dp/dt$ is the law.

3.3 Impulse

$\vec J = \vec F_{\text{avg}}\,\Delta t = \Delta\vec p = \displaystyle\int\vec F\,dt$  — unit N s = kg m s−1. For a varying force, impulse is the area under the $F$–$t$ graph.

The whole point of impulse: $\Delta p$ is often fixed by the situation, so a longer $\Delta t$ means a smaller peak force. A cricketer catching a ball must bring its momentum to zero either way; pulling the hands back stretches $\Delta t$ and cuts the force. Same physics: bending your knees on landing, airbags, sand pits under a high-jump bar, foam packaging.

Worked example — impulse with a reversal

A ball of mass 0.15 kg travelling at 20 m s−1 strikes a wall perpendicularly and rebounds along the same line at 15 m s−1. Contact lasts 0.02 s. Find the change in momentum and the average force.

Take "toward the wall" as positive. This single decision is where the marks are.

$p_i = 0.15 \times (+20) = +3.00$ kg m s−1    $p_f = 0.15 \times (-15) = -2.25$ kg m s−1

$\Delta p = p_f - p_i = -2.25 - 3.00 = -5.25$ kg m s−1

$F = \dfrac{\Delta p}{\Delta t} = \dfrac{5.25}{0.02} = 262.5$ N

Δp = 5.25 kg m s⁻¹ directed away from the wall; F = 262.5 N.

If you forget that the rebound velocity is negative you get 0.75 instead of 5.25. That one sign is the most common 3-mark loss in this chapter.

4 · Newton's Third Law

Statement. To every action there is always an equal and opposite reaction. Stated properly: Force on A by B = − Force on B by A.

The statement is easy; the interpretation is where marks are lost. Non-negotiable points:

  • The two forces act on two DIFFERENT bodies. Therefore they can never cancel each other. Cancellation only ever happens between forces acting on the same body.
  • They are simultaneous. There is no "action first, then reaction". The labels "action" and "reaction" are arbitrary — neither is more fundamental.
  • They are always of the same type — both gravitational, or both contact forces, and so on.

4.1 The classic trap — the book on the table

A book rests on a table. The normal force $N$ (table pushes book up) and the weight $mg$ (Earth pulls book down) are equal and opposite — but they are NOT a third-law pair. Both act on the same body, the book. They are a first-law balance.

The true partners are:

ForceIts third-law reactionActs on
Table pushes book up ($N$)Book pushes table downthe table
Earth pulls book down ($mg$)Book pulls Earth upthe Earth
Frequently asked
"What is the reaction to the weight of the book?" The answer is not the normal force. It is the upward gravitational pull the book exerts on the Earth. A useful test: removing the table destroys the first pair but leaves the second untouched.

4.2 The horse-and-cart objection

"If the cart pulls back on the horse exactly as hard as the horse pulls the cart, how can anything move?"

Because those two forces act on different bodies, so they never appear in the same equation. To find the horse's motion, draw only the forces acting on the horse: the cart pulls it backward, and the ground pushes it forward (friction). The ground force is larger, so the horse accelerates.

Two are worth a second look. In the Earth–Moon case the forces really are equal — the Moon's pull on the Earth is what raises the tides. And in walking, the reaction is a friction force: on ice you push the ground backward just as hard, but the ground cannot push you forward, so you go nowhere.

5 · Conservation of linear momentum

This is not an independent law. It follows from the second and third laws together, and the derivation is examinable.

DERIVATION 2  Conservation of momentum, and gun recoil
  1. Take two bodies $A$ and $B$ forming an isolated system — no external force acts. They interact with each other for a time $\Delta t$.
  2. Third law: the force on $A$ due to $B$ and on $B$ due to $A$ satisfy $\vec F_{AB} = -\vec F_{BA}$.
  3. Second law applied to each: $\dfrac{d\vec p_A}{dt} = \vec F_{AB}$  and  $\dfrac{d\vec p_B}{dt} = \vec F_{BA}$.
  4. Add the two equations: $\dfrac{d}{dt}(\vec p_A + \vec p_B) = \vec F_{AB} + \vec F_{BA} = 0$.
  5. If the derivative of a quantity is zero, the quantity is constant: $\vec p_A + \vec p_B = \text{constant}$.
  6. Gun recoil. Gun (mass $M$) and bullet (mass $m$) are initially at rest, so total momentum $= 0$. After firing, $0 = m\vec v_b + M\vec v_g$.
  7. $\Rightarrow \vec v_g = -\dfrac{m}{M}\vec v_b$ — the recoil is opposite to the bullet and small in magnitude because $M \gg m$.
$\vec F_{\text{ext}} = 0 \Rightarrow m_1\vec u_1 + m_2\vec u_2 = m_1\vec v_1 + m_2\vec v_2$

Read the condition carefully: $F_{\text{external}} = 0$. During a collision the internal forces are enormous but external forces (gravity, friction) are negligible over the tiny contact time — which is why momentum is conserved in collisions even on a rough floor.

6 · Equilibrium of a particle

$\sum\vec F = 0$, which for concurrent forces means $\sum F_x = 0$ and $\sum F_y = 0$.

Three concurrent forces in equilibrium form a closed triangle when drawn head to tail. For exactly three forces you may use Lami's theorem:

$\dfrac{F_1}{\sin\alpha} = \dfrac{F_2}{\sin\beta} = \dfrac{F_3}{\sin\gamma}$ — where each angle is the one opposite to its force, i.e. the angle between the other two.

7 · The forces you will actually meet

ForceDirectionKey point
Weight $mg$Vertically down, acts at the centre of massNever changes with the motion of the body
Normal reaction $N$Perpendicular to the contact surfaceAlways a push, never a pull. $N$ is not always $mg$
Tension $T$Along the string, away from the bodyAlways a pull. Uniform throughout only if the string is massless and the pulley is massless and frictionless
Spring forceOpposite to the deformation$F = -kx$, restoring
Friction $f$Parallel to the surfaceOpposes relative sliding, or the tendency to slide
The single most useful sentence in this chapter: $N$ equals $mg$ only on flat ground with no vertical component of any applied force. On an incline $N = mg\cos\theta$; in a lift $N = m(g\pm a)$; with a force pulled at angle $\theta$ above the horizontal, $N = mg - F\sin\theta$.

Conceptually, normal force, tension and friction are all contact forces, and all of them are electromagnetic in origin at the microscopic level.

8 · Friction — the hardest section in the chapter

8.1 The three kinds

Static friction ($f_s$) acts when there is no relative sliding, only a tendency to slide. Its defining property is that it is self-adjusting.

Push a heavy box with 10 N and it does not move — friction is exactly 10 N. Push with 30 N, still no motion — friction is exactly 30 N. It matches whatever you apply, up to a ceiling:

$f_s \le \mu_s N$, with maximum (limiting) value $(f_s)_{\max} = \mu_s N$

Writing $f_s = \mu_s N$ when the body is not on the verge of slipping is a straight error.

Limiting friction is that maximum static value — the friction acting at the exact instant slipping begins.

Kinetic friction acts once sliding has started: $f_k = \mu_k N$. It is roughly constant, independent of the sliding speed and of the apparent area of contact. And $\mu_k < \mu_s$, which is why a stuck box lurches forward the instant it gives — the opposing force suddenly drops.

Rolling friction is far smaller than sliding friction. That is the entire reason for wheels and ball bearings.

8.2 What μ depends on

The coefficient of friction depends on exactly two things:

  1. The nature of the two surfaces in contact — how rough or smooth they are.
  2. The materials of the bodies in contact.

It does not depend on the area of contact, the normal force, the mass, or the speed of sliding.

8.3 Angle of friction and angle of repose

Angle of friction $\lambda$ is the angle between the normal reaction $N$ and the resultant of $N$ and the limiting friction $f_{\max}$.

$\tan\lambda = \dfrac{f_{\max}}{N} = \dfrac{\mu_s N}{N}$  $\Rightarrow$  $\boxed{\tan\lambda = \mu_s}$

Angle of repose $\alpha$ is the inclination at which a body placed on a slope just begins to slide. On the verge, the component of weight along the slope equals limiting friction:

$mg\sin\alpha = \mu_s\,mg\cos\alpha$  $\Rightarrow$  $\boxed{\tan\alpha = \mu_s}$  — note the mass cancels.

$\lambda = \alpha$. Same number, two different physical set-ups. Both equal $\mu_s$.

8.4 The decision procedure that fixes most friction errors

  1. Find $N$ first, from the direction perpendicular to the surface. Flat ground, no vertical force: $N = mg$. Incline $\theta$: $N = mg\cos\theta$. Force pulled at $\theta$ above horizontal: $N = mg - F\sin\theta$. Pushed at $\theta$ below: $N = mg + F\sin\theta$.
  2. Compare. Work out $f_{\max} = \mu_s N$ and compare with the force applied along the surface.
    • Applied $<$ $f_{\max}$ → static. $f$ = the applied force. $a = 0$.
    • Applied $>$ $f_{\max}$ → sliding. $f = \mu_k N$.
  3. Direction: opposite to how that surface slides, or would slide if the contact suddenly turned to frictionless ice.

Almost every friction mistake is skipping step 1 and assuming $N = mg$.

8.5 Which way does friction act?

Friction opposes relative sliding between the two surfaces — not the motion of the body through space. That is why friction can be the force that drives motion.

Three cases with the same rule:

  • You push a crate along a floor. The crate slides forward over the floor, so friction on the crate acts backward. The familiar case.
  • A crate sits on the bed of an accelerating truck. If the bed were ice, the crate would be left behind — it would slip backward relative to the bed. So friction on the crate acts forward, and it is the only force accelerating the crate.
  • A car's driving wheel. The tyre surface pushes the road backward, so the road pushes the tyre forward. This is what moves the car.

A faster test when friction is the only horizontal force: its direction is forced by $F = ma$. The block on the truck bed accelerates forward, so the net force on it points forward, so friction points forward. No intuition required.

Friction is not always horizontal. A block pressed against a vertical wall tends to slide down, so friction on it acts vertically upward.

8.6 Why pulling beats pushing

This is a standard 2-mark question ("why is it easier to pull a lawn roller than to push it?").

  • Pushing at an angle $\theta$ below the horizontal: the vertical component presses the roller down, so $N = mg + F\sin\theta$. Larger $N$ ⟹ larger friction ⟹ harder.
  • Pulling at an angle $\theta$ above the horizontal: the vertical component lifts part of the weight, so $N = mg - F\sin\theta$. Smaller $N$ ⟹ smaller friction ⟹ easier.
Worked example — pulling versus pushing

A 20 kg box on a floor with $\mu_k = 0.25$ is acted on by a 100 N force at 30° to the horizontal. Find the acceleration when the force is (a) pulling upward, (b) pushing downward. Take $g = 10$ m s−2.

(a) Pulling. Vertically there is no acceleration, so the vertical forces balance:

$N + F\sin30^\circ = mg \Rightarrow N = 200 - 50 = 150$ N  →  $f = 0.25\times150 = 37.5$ N

Horizontally: $F\cos30^\circ - f = ma \Rightarrow 86.60 - 37.5 = 20a$

a = 2.46 m s⁻²

(b) Pushing. $N = mg + F\sin30^\circ = 250$ N  →  $f = 62.5$ N

$86.60 - 62.5 = 20a$

a = 1.21 m s⁻²

Same 100 N force, half the acceleration. This only shows up if you compute $N$ properly instead of assuming $N = mg$.

9 · Circular motion dynamics

"Centripetal force" is not a new force. It is the name for the net inward force, whatever supplies it — tension for a stone on a string, gravity for a satellite, friction for a car on a flat road, a component of $N$ for a banked road. Never draw a separate arrow labelled "centripetal force" on a free-body diagram.

$F_c = \dfrac{mv^2}{r} = m\omega^2 r$

DERIVATION 3  Maximum speed on a level circular road
  1. A car of mass $m$ rounds a horizontal circular road of radius $R$. The forces on it are its weight $mg$ down, the normal reaction $N$ up, and static friction $f$ — the only horizontal force available.
  2. Vertical equilibrium: $N = mg$.
  3. Friction must supply the whole centripetal force: $\dfrac{mv^2}{R} = f \le \mu_s N = \mu_s mg$.
  4. Divide through by $m$: $\dfrac{v^2}{R} \le \mu_s g$.
$v_{\max} = \sqrt{\mu_s R g}$ — independent of the mass of the car. Beyond it the car skids outward.
DERIVATION 4  Banking of roads — optimum and maximum speed

Meaning. Banking is raising the outer edge of a curved road above the inner edge. Why it is needed: relying on friction alone to supply the centripetal force is dangerous (friction fails when the road is wet) and wears the tyres rapidly. Banking lets the horizontal component of the normal reaction supply the force instead.

  1. Frictionless case first. Road banked at angle $\theta$, radius $R$. Only two forces act: $N$ perpendicular to the road surface, and $mg$ vertically down.
  2. Resolve $N$. Vertical (no vertical acceleration): $N\cos\theta = mg$.
  3. Horizontal (toward the centre, supplying $F_c$): $N\sin\theta = \dfrac{mv_0^2}{R}$.
  4. Divide the second by the first — $N$ and $m$ both cancel: $\tan\theta = \dfrac{v_0^2}{Rg}$.
  5. $\Rightarrow v_0 = \sqrt{Rg\tan\theta}$  and  $\theta = \tan^{-1}\!\left(\dfrac{v_0^2}{Rg}\right)$. This is the optimum speed — at exactly this speed no friction is needed at all, so tyre wear is minimised.
  6. With friction, at maximum speed the car tends to slip up and outward, so friction $f = \mu_s N$ acts down the incline. Resolving:
  7. Vertical: $N\cos\theta = mg + f\sin\theta$  ·  Horizontal: $N\sin\theta + f\cos\theta = \dfrac{mv_{\max}^2}{R}$
  8. Put $f = \mu_s N$ and divide the horizontal equation by the vertical one to eliminate $N$ and $m$: $\dfrac{v_{\max}^2}{Rg} = \dfrac{\sin\theta+\mu_s\cos\theta}{\cos\theta-\mu_s\sin\theta} = \dfrac{\tan\theta+\mu_s}{1-\mu_s\tan\theta}$
$v_0 = \sqrt{Rg\tan\theta}$  ·  $v_{\max}=\sqrt{Rg\dfrac{\mu_s+\tan\theta}{1-\mu_s\tan\theta}}$  ·  $v_{\min}=\sqrt{Rg\dfrac{\tan\theta-\mu_s}{1+\mu_s\tan\theta}}$ (real only if $\tan\theta>\mu_s$)
Checks: $\mu_s = 0$ gives $\sqrt{Rg\tan\theta}$ ✓   $\theta = 0$ gives $\sqrt{\mu_s Rg}$, the level road ✓

10 · Standard systems

10.1 Apparent weight in a lift

Solve these in the ground frame and nothing is mysterious. The true weight $mg$ never changes. What changes is $N$, the normal reaction — which is exactly what a weighing scale reads.

SituationEquationScale reads
At rest or constant velocity$N - mg = 0$$N = mg$
Accelerating up with $a$$N - mg = ma$$N = m(g+a)$
Accelerating down with $a$$mg - N = ma$$N = m(g-a)$
Free fall ($a = g$)$mg - N = mg$$N = 0$ — weightlessness
Downward $a > g$—Body leaves the floor and presses on the roof

Weightlessness is not the absence of gravity. In free fall gravity still pulls with the full $mg$ — that is exactly what produces the acceleration. What vanishes is the floor's push, and since that is all your body can feel, you feel weightless.

10.2 Atwood machine

DERIVATION 5  Atwood machine — acceleration and tension
  1. Masses $m_1 > m_2$ hang from a light inextensible string over a frictionless massless pulley. Because the string cannot stretch, both have the same magnitude of acceleration; because the pulley is ideal, $T$ is the same on both sides.
  2. For $m_1$, taking downward as positive: $m_1g - T = m_1a$.
  3. For $m_2$, taking upward as positive: $T - m_2g = m_2a$.
  4. Add the two equations — $T$ cancels: $(m_1 - m_2)g = (m_1+m_2)a$.
  5. Substitute back into either equation for $T$.
$a = \dfrac{(m_1-m_2)g}{m_1+m_2}$  ·  $T = \dfrac{2m_1m_2 g}{m_1+m_2}$  ·  force on the pulley $= 2T$
Check: $T$ must lie between the two weights, $m_2g < T < m_1g$.

10.4 Connected bodies

  • Two blocks in contact on a smooth floor, $F$ applied to $m_1$: $a = \dfrac{F}{m_1+m_2}$, and the contact force is $\dfrac{m_2F}{m_1+m_2}$. The rule: the contact force equals (the mass ahead of the contact) × $a$. Push the other block instead and the answer changes.
  • Two blocks joined by a string, pulled by $F$: $a = \dfrac{F}{m_1+m_2}$, and $T = \dfrac{m_{\text{behind}}}{m_1+m_2}F$.
Worked example — trolley and hanging block with friction

A 20 kg trolley on a table ($\mu_k = 0.04$) is connected over a frictionless pulley to a 3 kg block hanging freely. Find the acceleration and the tension. Take $g = 10$ m s−2.

Friction on the trolley: $N = 20\times10 = 200$ N, so $f_k = 0.04\times200 = 8$ N.

Hanging block (down positive): $30 - T = 3a$  … (1)

Trolley (right positive): $T - 8 = 20a$  … (2)

Adding: $30 - 8 = 23a \Rightarrow a = \dfrac{22}{23}$

a ≈ 0.957 m s⁻² · T = 20(22/23) + 8 = 624/23 ≈ 27.1 N

11 · The method — use it every single time

  1. Pick one body and say out loud what it is. One body, one diagram.
  2. Draw only the forces acting ON it. Weight always. Then one arrow per surface it touches and per string attached. Forces it exerts on other things belong on their diagrams.
  3. Fix the friction direction with the ice test: if this contact were frictionless, which way would this surface slip? Friction points opposite.
  4. Choose axes intelligently — along and perpendicular to the slope for inclines, along the acceleration otherwise. Resolve whatever does not lie on an axis, which is usually just $mg$.
  5. Write $\sum F = ma$ per direction. Use $\sum F = 0$ in any direction with no acceleration — that is nearly always how you find $N$, and $N$ is nearly always what friction depends on.
  6. Check the limits. Does $\mu = 0$ give the frictionless answer? Does the tension sit between the two weights? Does the mass cancel where it should?
Traps that cost marks
  1. Action and reaction act on different bodies, so they never cancel and must never both appear in one free-body diagram. $N$ and $mg$ on a resting block are not a pair.
  2. On an incline $N = mg\cos\theta$, so friction is $\mu\,mg\cos\theta$ — not $\mu\,mg$.
  3. $f_s = \mu_s N$ is the maximum only. Below the limit, static friction equals whatever force is applied — possibly zero. Always compare the applied force with $f_{\max}$ before choosing a formula.
  4. In a lift, the true weight $mg$ never changes — only $N$ does. In free fall $N = 0$, yet $g$ still acts.
  5. $\sqrt{Rg\tan\theta}$ is the optimum speed, not the maximum. With friction there is a whole band $v_{\min} \le v \le v_{\max}$.
  6. Tension is uniform only for a massless string over a massless frictionless pulley.
  7. Never draw "centripetal force" as an extra arrow. Name the real force and set it equal to $mv^2/r$.
  8. Signs in impulse problems. A rebounding velocity is negative. Fix a positive direction before you write anything down.
