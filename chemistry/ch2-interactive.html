Section 0How the chapter hangs together

This is not a list of facts. It is a chain of four models, each killed by one specific experiment. If you can draw the chain, you can answer most of the theory questions without memorising them separately.

The one sentence that organises everything

Classical physics says energy is continuous and a charged particle in orbit must radiate. Atoms are stable and emit discrete lines. Every idea in this chapter — Planck's quantum, the photon, Bohr's stationary states, quantised angular momentum — exists to fix that one contradiction.

The three experiments, one line each

  • Cathode rays → the electron. Rays from the cathode bend towards the positive plate, so they are negative. Their e/m is the same whatever gas or electrode is used — so the electron is universal to all matter. Thomson measured e/m = 1.758820×10¹¹ C kg⁻¹.
  • Millikan's oil drop → the charge. Charge on a drop is always an integral multiple of 1.602×10⁻¹⁹ C, so charge is quantised. Combining with Thomson: mₑ = e ÷ (e/m) = 9.1094×10⁻³¹ kg.
  • Anode (canal) rays → the proton. Positive, and their e/m depends on the gas — largest for hydrogen, giving the lightest positive particle.
Exam angle

"Why is e/m of cathode rays independent of the gas, but of anode rays dependent on it?" Because the electron is a constituent of every atom, so it is always the same particle; the positive ion left behind is whatever the gas happened to be, so its mass changes.

Section 2The models that failed

Thomson (1898) — and why one experiment ended it

A uniform sphere of positive charge with electrons embedded in it: the "plum pudding" or "watermelon" model. It explained electrical neutrality and nothing else. Because the positive charge was spread thinly over the whole atom, no α-particle should ever have been deflected sharply — yet some were.

Rutherford's α-scattering (1911)

A thin gold foil (~100 nm) bombarded with α-particles (He²⁺) from a radioactive source, detected on a fluorescent ZnS screen. Three observations, three conclusions — and the exam always wants them paired.

Rutherford's two fatal drawbacks — a guaranteed question
  1. Stability. By classical electrodynamics, an accelerating charge continuously radiates energy. An electron in a circular orbit is always accelerating (its direction changes), so it must spiral into the nucleus in about 10⁻⁸ s. Matter could not exist.
  2. Spectra. A continuously spiralling electron would lose energy continuously and emit a continuous spectrum. Atoms actually give sharp line spectra.

It also said nothing about how electrons are arranged, and therefore nothing about chemistry.

Section 3Photons and the photoelectric effect

Planck's quantum theory (1900)

Classical theory predicted a black body would radiate ever more strongly at short wavelengths — the "ultraviolet catastrophe". The observed curve instead peaks and falls. Planck fixed it by abandoning continuous energy.

Learn this

Energy is emitted or absorbed only in discrete packets — quanta — of energy

E = hν = hc/λ    h = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J s

and total energy is always an integral multiple nhν. For light, one quantum is a photon. Useful shortcut: E(eV) = 1240 / λ(nm).

The photoelectric effect (Hertz 1887, explained by Einstein 1905)

Shine light on a metal surface; above a certain frequency, electrons are ejected. Four observations — and the wave theory of light fails on all four.

Derivation 1 · Einstein's photoelectric equation
  1. A single photon of energy hν is absorbed by a single electron.
  2. Part of it, the work function φ = hν₀, is spent freeing the electron from the metal surface.
  3. Conservation of energy gives the remainder to the electron as kinetic energy:
  4. hν = hν₀ + ½mₑv²
  5. If a retarding potential V₀ just stops the fastest electron, eV₀ = ½mₑv², so eV₀ = h(ν − ν₀).
KEmax = hν − hν₀ = h(ν − ν₀). A graph of KE against ν is a straight line of slope h, with x-intercept ν₀ — the same slope for every metal, because h is universal.

The three traps here
  1. Intensity never ejects an electron below ν₀. Brighter light means more photons, not more energetic ones. Drag the slider past the threshold and watch the verdict flip — intensity is not even an input.
  2. The slope is h for every metal. Switch metals: the line shifts sideways, never tilts. Different metals give parallel lines.

Section 4Atomic spectra and the hydrogen lines

Three kinds of spectrum

  • Continuous — white light through a prism. No gaps; every wavelength present.
  • Emission line — bright lines on a dark background. Produced by excited atoms falling back down. A fingerprint of the element.
  • Absorption line — dark lines on a continuous background, at exactly the same positions. The atom absorbs precisely what it would emit.

Rydberg's formula

Purely empirical when written in 1888 — it just fitted the observed lines. Bohr derived it 25 years later, and that is why his model was believed.

ν̄ = 1/λ = 109677 (1/n₁² − 1/n₂²) cm⁻¹

with n₂ > n₁. The series is named by where the electron lands (n₁), never where it starts.

Mnemonic and the two facts that go with it

Lyman Balmer Paschen Brackett Pfund → "Light Bulbs Produce Bright Photons", with n₁ = 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 in that order.

  • Only Balmer is visible. Lyman is always UV (biggest energy gaps); everything from Paschen out is IR.
  • Within a series, the first line (n₁+1 → n₁) has the smallest energy and longest wavelength; the series limit (∞ → n₁) has the largest energy and shortest wavelength.
The counting question

"How many spectral lines appear when an electron de-excites from level n to the ground state?" — n(n−1)/2. From n = 4: 4×3/2 = 6 lines. If it falls only as far as level n₁ instead of the ground state, use (n₂−n₁)(n₂−n₁+1)/2.

Section 5Bohr's model

The highest-scoring section of the chapter. A five-mark derivation, a set of standard numericals, and a guaranteed "limitations" question all live here.

The four postulates

  1. The electron revolves in certain fixed circular orbits of definite energy, called stationary states.
  2. While in a stationary state the electron does not radiate energy. This is a bare postulate — introduced precisely to defeat the classical spiral-death objection.
  3. Energy is emitted or absorbed only when the electron jumps between states: ΔE = E₂ − E₁ = hν (Bohr's frequency rule).
  4. Angular momentum is quantised: mvr = nh/2π, n = 1, 2, 3 …
Derivation 2 · Radius and energy of the nth orbit

Part A — radius.

  1. Electrostatic attraction supplies the centripetal force:
    (1/4πε₀)(Ze²/r²) = mv²/r  ⟹  mv² = Ze²/4πε₀r  …(i)
  2. Quantised angular momentum: mvr = nh/2π ⟹ v = nh/2πmr  …(ii)
  3. Substitute (ii) into (i): m(nh/2πmr)² = Ze²/4πε₀r
  4. n²h²/4π²mr² = Ze²/4πε₀r ⟹ r = n²h²ε₀/πmZe²
rn = 52.9 (n²/Z) pm = 0.529 (n²/Z) Å  — so r ∝ n² and r ∝ 1/Z.

Part B — energy.

  1. From (i), KE = ½mv² = Ze²/8πε₀r
  2. PE = −Ze²/4πε₀r — negative, because the force is attractive.
  3. E = KE + PE = Ze²/8πε₀r − Ze²/4πε₀r = −Ze²/8πε₀r
  4. Insert r from Part A: En = −mZ²e⁴ / 8ε₀²h²n²
En = −2.18×10⁻¹⁸ (Z²/n²) J = −13.6 (Z²/n²) eV. Also PE = 2E and KE = −E, so total energy is negative — which is exactly what "bound" means. And vn = 2.188×10⁶ (Z/n) m s⁻¹.
Derivation 3 · The Rydberg formula, from Bohr
  1. The electron falls n₂ → n₁, emitting one photon: ΔE = En₂ − En₁
  2. ΔE = 2.18×10⁻¹⁸ (1/n₁² − 1/n₂²) J
  3. By the frequency rule ΔE = hν, so ν = 3.29×10¹⁵ (1/n₁² − 1/n₂²) Hz
  4. Wavenumber ν̄ = ν/c = ΔE/hc
ν̄ = 109677 (1/n₁² − 1/n₂²) cm⁻¹ — Rydberg's empirical constant, now computed from m, e, h and ε₀ alone. That agreement is why the model was accepted.

Limitations of Bohr's model — five, and the exam wants all five
  1. Works only for one-electron species (H, He⁺, Li²⁺, Be³⁺). It ignores electron–electron repulsion, so it fails from helium onward.
  2. Cannot explain the fine structure of spectral lines seen with better spectrometers.
  3. Cannot explain line splitting in a magnetic field (Zeeman effect) or an electric field (Stark effect).
  4. Says nothing about molecular shapes or chemical bonding.
  5. It violates the Heisenberg uncertainty principle — a fixed orbit assigns an exact position and an exact momentum at the same time, which is not allowed.

Section 6de Broglie and Heisenberg

Derivation 4 · The de Broglie relation
  1. For a photon, Planck gives E = hν = hc/λ, and Einstein gives E = mc².
  2. Equate them: mc² = hc/λ ⟹ λ = h/mc = h/p.
  3. de Broglie's leap: if radiation can behave as a particle, matter can behave as a wave. Replace c by the particle's own speed v.
λ = h/mv = h/p. For an electron accelerated through V volts, λ = h/√(2mₑeV) ≈ 12.27/√V Å. Confirmed by Davisson and Germer (1927), who diffracted electrons off a nickel crystal — diffraction is a purely wave phenomenon.

The standard three-mark answer

"Why is the wave nature of a moving cricket ball not observed?" Because λ = h/mv is inversely proportional to mass. For a 0.1 kg ball at 10 m s⁻¹, λ ≈ 6.6×10⁻³⁴ m — smaller than any aperture that exists, so no diffraction can be detected. Wave character only matters for particles of very small mass.

Heisenberg's uncertainty principle (1927)

Statement

It is impossible in principle to determine simultaneously the exact position and the exact momentum of an electron:

Δx · Δp ≥ h/4π    or    Δx · Δv ≥ h/4πm

This is not an instrument limitation — it follows from the wave nature of matter. Its first casualty is the word "orbit": a precise path needs precise position and momentum together, which Bohr's model assumes.

Derivation 5 · Why an electron cannot live inside the nucleus
  1. Confining the electron to a nucleus means Δx ≈ 10⁻¹⁴ m (nuclear diameter).
  2. Δv ≥ h/(4πmΔx) = 6.626×10⁻³⁴ / (4π × 9.11×10⁻³¹ × 10⁻¹⁴)
  3. Δv ≥ 5.8×10⁹ m s⁻¹
That exceeds the speed of light (3×10⁸ m s⁻¹), which is impossible — so an electron cannot be confined inside the nucleus.

One elegant consequence worth quoting: Bohr's fourth postulate stops being arbitrary. A stable orbit must fit a whole number of electron wavelengths, 2πr = nλ = nh/mv, which rearranges to exactly mvr = nh/2π.

Section 7Quantum numbers

Section 8Orbital shapes, nodes and energies

Nodes — three formulae, one check

Angular (planar) nodes = l  ·  Radial (spherical) nodes = n − l − 1  ·  Total = n − 1. The third is your check: the first two must add to it. A 3s has 2 radial nodes and 0 angular; a 3p has 1 and 1; a 3d has 0 and 2. All total 2.

Why orbital energies split in a many-electron atom

In hydrogen (one electron) energy depends on n alone, so 2s = 2p and 3s = 3p = 3d are degenerate. In every other atom electron–electron repulsion splits them, and energy depends on both n and l.

Two competing effects do it. Shielding: inner electrons screen the nucleus, so an outer electron feels a reduced effective nuclear charge Zeff. Penetration: within a shell, an s electron spends more time close to the nucleus than p, which does more than d, which does more than f. More penetration → less shielding → larger Zeff → lower energy. Hence s < p < d < f within a shell.

The (n + l) rule

Lower (n + l) fills first. On a tie, the one with the lower n fills first. So 4s (4+0 = 4) fills before 3d (3+2 = 5); and 3d (5) fills before 4p (5) because 3d has the smaller n.

Section 9Filling rules and electronic configuration

Aufbau principle

Electrons occupy the lowest-energy orbital available first, in the (n+l) order.

Pauli exclusion principle

No two electrons in an atom can have all four quantum numbers the same — so an orbital holds at most two electrons, with opposite spins.

Hund's rule of maximum multiplicity

Pairing in the orbitals of a subshell does not begin until each orbital of that subshell has one electron, and all singly occupied orbitals have parallel spins. Note the wording: a subshell, not a shell.

Where marks go in this section
  1. Electrons leave from the outermost shell — 4s before 3d. Fe is [Ar]3d⁶4s², but Fe²⁺ is [Ar]3d⁶, not [Ar]3d⁴4s². Same for Cu²⁺ = [Ar]3d⁹.
  2. Configurations are written in shell order but filled in energy order. Both conventions are accepted; be consistent within one answer.
  3. Hund's rule applies within a subshell. 2p³ is three parallel unpaired electrons, one per orbital — never a pair plus a single.
  4. An orbital holds at most 2 electrons; only a Bohr shell holds 2n².

Section 10Question bank

Thirty-four questions in the shapes this chapter is actually set in. Try each one before opening it — reading answers you did not attempt is the least efficient revision there is.

1Which of these sets of quantum numbers is not possible: (a) n=3, l=2, mₗ=−2  (b) n=2, l=2, mₗ=0  (c) n=4, l=0, mₗ=0?

(b) is impossible. l can only run from 0 to (n−1), so for n=2 the allowed values are l = 0 and 1 only. l = 2 would mean a "2d" orbital, which does not exist.

1How many unpaired electrons does chromium have?

Six. Cr (Z=24) is [Ar]3d⁵4s¹ — five parallel electrons in 3d plus one in 4s, all unpaired.

1What is the orbital angular momentum of an electron in a 2s orbital?

Zero. Orbital angular momentum = √(l(l+1))·h/2π, and for an s orbital l = 0. Note this is a favourite trap — the answer is not h/2π.

1Give the total number of nodes in a 4d orbital, and split them into radial and angular.

Total = n − 1 = 3. Angular = l = 2. Radial = n − l − 1 = 4 − 2 − 1 = 1. Check: 2 + 1 = 3 ✓

1Which series of the hydrogen spectrum lies in the visible region, and why is it the only one?

Balmer (n₁ = 2). Lyman transitions end at n = 1, where the energy gaps are far larger, putting them in the ultraviolet; Paschen and beyond end at n ≥ 3 where the gaps are much smaller, putting them in the infrared. Only n₁ = 2 gives gaps of roughly 1.9–3.4 eV, which is visible light.

1Write the electronic configuration of Cu²⁺.

Cu (Z=29) is [Ar]3d¹⁰4s¹ (an exception). Removing two electrons takes the 4s electron first, then one 3d: Cu²⁺ = [Ar]3d⁹.

1What is the maximum number of electrons that can have n = 3 and l = 2?

l = 2 is the 3d subshell, with 2l+1 = 5 orbitals × 2 electrons = 10 electrons.

1Which transition emits the photon of higher energy: 3 → 1 or 3 → 2?

3 → 1. ΔE = 13.6(1/n₁² − 1/n₂²): for 3→1 that is 13.6(1 − 1/9) = 12.09 eV; for 3→2 it is 13.6(1/4 − 1/9) = 1.89 eV. Lyman transitions are always the most energetic.

1Define threshold frequency.

The minimum frequency of incident radiation below which no photoelectric emission occurs, however intense the radiation. It relates to the work function by φ = hν₀.

1An element has the configuration 1s²2s²2p⁶3s²3p³. How many electrons have l = 1?

l = 1 means p orbitals: 2p⁶ and 3p³ → 6 + 3 = 9 electrons.

2Calculate the de Broglie wavelength of an electron accelerated through a potential difference of 100 V.

λ = h/√(2mₑeV) = 12.27/√V Å = 12.27/√100 = 12.27/10 = 1.227 Å = 1.227 × 10⁻¹⁰ m.

This is comparable to atomic spacing in a crystal, which is exactly why electron diffraction (Davisson–Germer) works.

2Calculate the energy of one photon of light of wavelength 4000 Å, in joules and in eV.

λ = 4000 Å = 4.0 × 10⁻⁷ m.

E = hc/λ = (6.626×10⁻³⁴ × 3×10⁸)/(4.0×10⁻⁷) = 1.9878×10⁻²⁵ / 4.0×10⁻⁷ = 4.97 × 10⁻¹⁹ J

In eV: 4.97×10⁻¹⁹ / 1.602×10⁻¹⁹ = 3.10 eV. (Shortcut: 1240/400 nm = 3.10 eV.)

2How many spectral lines are produced when an electron in a hydrogen atom drops from n = 5 to the ground state?

n(n−1)/2 = 5 × 4 / 2 = 10 lines.

2Compare the radius and the energy of He⁺ in n = 2 with hydrogen in n = 1.

Radius: r = 52.9 n²/Z. He⁺: 52.9 × 4/2 = 105.8 pm. H: 52.9 × 1/1 = 52.9 pm. He⁺ is twice as large.

Energy: E = −13.6 Z²/n². He⁺: −13.6 × 4/4 = −13.6 eV. H: −13.6 eV. Identical.

The trap is assuming that equal energy implies equal size. Check n²/Z and Z²/n² separately.

2State Hund's rule and apply it to nitrogen.

Pairing of electrons in the orbitals of a given subshell does not occur until each orbital of that subshell has one electron, and all such singly occupied orbitals have parallel spins.

N (Z=7) = 1s² 2s² 2p³. The three 2p electrons occupy 2px, 2py, 2pz singly with parallel spins — giving three unpaired electrons, not one pair and one single.

2Why are 2s and 2p degenerate in hydrogen but not in lithium?

Hydrogen has only one electron, so there is no electron–electron repulsion and the energy depends on n alone — 2s and 2p are degenerate.

In lithium there are inner (1s) electrons. The 2s orbital penetrates closer to the nucleus than 2p, so it is less shielded, experiences a larger Zeff, and is lowered in energy. Hence E(2s) < E(2p).

2Calculate the uncertainty in the velocity of an electron whose position is known to within 0.1 Å.

Δx = 0.1 Å = 1.0 × 10⁻¹¹ m.

Δv ≥ h/(4π m Δx) = 6.626×10⁻³⁴ / (4 × 3.14 × 9.11×10⁻³¹ × 1.0×10⁻¹¹)

= 6.626×10⁻³⁴ / (1.145×10⁻⁴¹) = 5.8 × 10⁶ m s⁻¹ — about 2% of the speed of light, for an atom-sized uncertainty in position.

3The work function of caesium is 1.9 eV. Find its threshold wavelength, and the kinetic energy of an electron ejected by 500 nm light.

Threshold: λ₀ = hc/φ = 1240/1.9 = 653 nm (using the eV·nm shortcut).

Photon energy at 500 nm: E = 1240/500 = 2.48 eV.

KE = E − φ = 2.48 − 1.90 = 0.58 eV = 9.3 × 10⁻²⁰ J.

Since 500 nm < 653 nm, emission does occur. Note that a longer wavelength than λ₀ would give nothing at all, whatever the intensity.

3Calculate the wavelength of the first line of the Balmer series.

First line means the smallest jump into n₁ = 2, i.e. n₂ = 3.

ν̄ = 109677 (1/2² − 1/3²) = 109677 (0.25 − 0.1111) = 109677 × 0.13889 = 15233 cm⁻¹

λ = 1/ν̄ = 1/15233 cm = 6.565 × 10⁻⁵ cm = 656.5 nm — the red Hα line.

3Why do chromium and copper not follow the Aufbau order?

Expected Cr = [Ar]3d⁴4s², actual [Ar]3d⁵4s¹. Expected Cu = [Ar]3d⁹4s², actual [Ar]3d¹⁰4s¹.

Because the 3d and 4s levels are very close in energy, and exactly half-filled (d⁵) or completely filled (d¹⁰) subshells carry extra stability from two causes:

  • Symmetrical distribution of electron density around the nucleus.
  • Exchange energy — energy released when electrons of parallel spin exchange positions. The greater the number of possible exchanges, the greater the stability, and a half-filled set maximises them.

3State the observations of Rutherford's α-scattering experiment and the conclusion drawn from each.
  • Most α-particles passed straight through the foil undeflected → the atom is mostly empty space.
  • A few were deflected through small angles → there is a concentrated positive charge occupying a very small volume.
  • Very few (about 1 in 20,000) rebounded through more than 90° → that small volume also carries almost the entire mass of the atom.

Conclusion: the nucleus, with radius ~10⁻¹⁵ m against an atomic radius of ~10⁻¹⁰ m.

3What are the four observations of the photoelectric effect that the wave theory of light cannot explain?
  • Existence of a threshold frequency below which no emission occurs, however intense the light.
  • Emission is instantaneous — no time lag while energy accumulates.
  • The kinetic energy of the ejected electrons depends on the frequency, not the intensity.
  • The number of electrons ejected (the photocurrent) depends on the intensity, not the frequency.
5Derive an expression for the radius and the energy of the nth Bohr orbit of a hydrogen-like species.

Radius. The electrostatic attraction supplies the centripetal force:

(1/4πε₀)(Ze²/r²) = mv²/r ⟹ mv² = Ze²/4πε₀r  …(i)

Bohr's quantisation of angular momentum: mvr = nh/2π ⟹ v = nh/2πmr  …(ii)

Substituting (ii) in (i): m(nh/2πmr)² = Ze²/4πε₀r, giving n²h²/4π²mr² = Ze²/4πε₀r

⟹ r = n²h²ε₀ / πmZe² = 52.9 (n²/Z) pm

Energy. KE = ½mv² = Ze²/8πε₀r (from (i)); PE = −Ze²/4πε₀r.

E = KE + PE = −Ze²/8πε₀r

Substituting r:

Eₙ = −mZ²e⁴/8ε₀²h²n² = −2.18×10⁻¹⁸ (Z²/n²) J = −13.6 (Z²/n²) eV

The negative sign shows the electron is bound; note also PE = 2E and KE = −E.

5Derive Rydberg's formula from Bohr's model, and use it to explain the spectral series of hydrogen.

An electron falling from n₂ to n₁ emits one photon of energy

ΔE = Eₙ₂ − Eₙ₁ = 2.18×10⁻¹⁸ (1/n₁² − 1/n₂²) J

By Bohr's frequency rule ΔE = hν, so ν = ΔE/h = 3.29×10¹⁵ (1/n₁² − 1/n₂²) Hz.

The wavenumber is ν̄ = ν/c = ΔE/hc:

ν̄ = 109677 (1/n₁² − 1/n₂²) cm⁻¹

This is exactly Rydberg's empirical formula, with RH now calculated from m, e, h and ε₀ alone.

Each value of n₁ generates one series: n₁ = 1 Lyman (UV), 2 Balmer (visible), 3 Paschen (IR), 4 Brackett (IR), 5 Pfund (far IR). Within a series, larger n₂ gives larger ΔE and shorter λ, converging to the series limit at n₂ = ∞.

5State and derive Einstein's photoelectric equation. Sketch the graph of kinetic energy against frequency for two different metals and explain its features.

A photon of energy hν is absorbed by a single electron. Energy φ = hν₀ (the work function) is used to free it from the surface; the rest becomes kinetic energy:

hν = hν₀ + ½mv²  ⟹  KEmax = h(ν − ν₀)

If a stopping potential V₀ just halts the fastest electron, eV₀ = h(ν − ν₀).

Graph: a straight line of KE against ν.

  • Slope = h, Planck's constant — the same for every metal, so different metals give parallel lines.
  • x-intercept = ν₀, the threshold frequency; a metal with a larger work function has its line shifted to the right.
  • Below ν₀ there is no emission at all, so the line does not continue below the axis.

The graph proves the quantum nature of light: a wave picture would make KE depend on intensity, giving no threshold and no such straight line.

5State the limitations of Bohr's model, and explain how de Broglie and Heisenberg led to its replacement.

Limitations:

  1. Applies only to one-electron species (H, He⁺, Li²⁺, Be³⁺); fails for multi-electron atoms because it ignores electron–electron repulsion.
  2. Cannot explain the fine structure of spectral lines.
  3. Cannot explain the Zeeman effect (splitting in a magnetic field) or the Stark effect (electric field).
  4. Gives no account of chemical bonding or molecular shape.
  5. Violates the uncertainty principle by fixing position and momentum together.

de Broglie (λ = h/mv) showed the electron has wave character, so treating it as a point on a circular track is wrong; a stable orbit must instead fit a whole number of wavelengths (2πr = nλ), which incidentally derives Bohr's fourth postulate rather than assuming it.

Heisenberg (Δx·Δp ≥ h/4π) showed that a definite orbit is not merely unknown but meaningless. Together these forced the replacement of the orbit with the orbital — a region of probability described by ψ², from Schrödinger's wave equation.

5Explain the four quantum numbers, their permitted values and their physical significance, and use them to justify the maximum electron capacity of the M shell.

n (principal) = 1, 2, 3 … Fixes the shell, the size and mainly the energy. Maximum electrons 2n².

l (azimuthal) = 0 to (n−1). Fixes the subshell and the shape; l = 0, 1, 2, 3 are s, p, d, f. Orbital angular momentum = √(l(l+1))·h/2π.

mₗ (magnetic) = −l to +l, giving (2l+1) values. Fixes the orientation of the orbital in space, and therefore the number of orbitals in the subshell.

mₛ (spin) = +½ or −½. Fixes the direction of electron spin; it is not a solution of the Schrödinger equation but is required by experiment.

M shell (n = 3): l can be 0, 1, 2 → subshells 3s, 3p, 3d, with 1, 3 and 5 orbitals respectively = 9 orbitals. By Pauli, each holds 2 electrons:

9 × 2 = 18 electrons = 2n² = 2(3)² ✓

A–RAssertion: The 2p orbital has one nodal plane. Reason: The number of angular nodes equals l.

Both assertion and reason are true, and the reason is the correct explanation. For a 2p orbital l = 1, so angular nodes = 1 — the plane through the nucleus perpendicular to the orbital axis, where the probability of finding the electron is zero.

A–RAssertion: Increasing the intensity of light always increases the kinetic energy of photoelectrons. Reason: Intensity is the number of photons striking per second.

The assertion is false; the reason is true. Intensity fixes how many photons arrive, and therefore the photocurrent — not how much energy each carries. Kinetic energy depends only on frequency, through KE = h(ν − ν₀).

A–RAssertion: Bohr's model can calculate the spectral lines of the He⁺ ion. Reason: Bohr's model applies to species with a single electron.

Both are true, and the reason correctly explains the assertion. He⁺ has one electron, so there is no electron–electron repulsion, and Bohr's expressions hold with Z = 2: Eₙ = −13.6 × 4/n² eV.

A–RAssertion: The energy of an electron in a hydrogen atom increases as n increases. Reason: Eₙ = −13.6/n² eV, so the value becomes less negative.

Both true, and the reason explains the assertion. "Higher energy" means less negative. The electron is less tightly bound at large n, and at n = ∞ the energy reaches 0, meaning the electron has escaped. The common error is reading the increasing magnitude of a negative number as increasing energy.

2Why is the (n+l) rule needed at all, and what happens when two orbitals have the same (n+l)?

Because in a multi-electron atom energy depends on both n and l, not on n alone, the shells overlap — 4s actually lies below 3d.

The rule: the orbital with the lower (n+l) fills first. On a tie, the one with the lower n fills first. Example: 3d (3+2 = 5) and 4p (4+1 = 5) tie, so 3d fills first because n = 3 < 4.

2A hydrogen atom in the ground state absorbs a photon of 12.09 eV. To which level is the electron promoted?

E₁ = −13.6 eV. After absorbing 12.09 eV: E = −13.6 + 12.09 = −1.51 eV.

Since Eₙ = −13.6/n², we need n² = 13.6/1.51 = 9, so n = 3.

3Calculate the ionisation energy of the He⁺ ion in eV and in kJ mol⁻¹.

Ionisation means n = 1 → n = ∞, so IE = 13.6 × Z² = 13.6 × 4 = 54.4 eV.

Per mole: 54.4 × 1.602×10⁻¹⁹ × 6.022×10²³ = 5.25 × 10⁶ J mol⁻¹ = 5250 kJ mol⁻¹.

Compare hydrogen at 13.6 eV (1312 kJ mol⁻¹): four times larger, because IE ∝ Z².

Section 11The last-hour sheet

If you read nothing else before walking in, read this. Every line is a mark that gets dropped every year.

Check these fifteen
  1. Photoelectric KE depends on frequency; photocurrent depends on intensity. No intensity below ν₀ ejects anything.
  2. Bohr works only for one-electron species — H, He⁺, Li²⁺, Be³⁺. Never for neutral lithium.
  3. A series is named by where the electron lands. n₁ = 2 is always Balmer, whatever n₂ is.
  4. Energy is negative and rises towards zero as n increases, and the gaps get smaller, never larger.
  5. Total nodes = n − 1, of which l are angular. A 3s has 2 radial nodes, not 3.
  6. Angular momentum of an s electron is zero, because l = 0. Not h/2π.
  7. l never equals n. 2d, 1p and 3f do not exist.
  8. In hydrogen 2s = 2p; in every other atom 2s < 2p. Questions specify the atom deliberately.
  9. mₗ counts orientations, not electrons. A p subshell has 3 orbitals and 6 electrons.
  10. An orbital holds 2 electrons; only a shell holds 2n².
  11. Cr is 3d⁵4s¹ and Cu is 3d¹⁰4s¹ — quote symmetry and exchange energy, both.
  12. 4s fills first but empties first. Fe²⁺ = [Ar]3d⁶, not [Ar]3d⁴4s².
  13. Isotopes differ in neutrons; isobars differ in protons. Do not swap them.
  14. He⁺ at n=2 has the same energy as H at n=1 but twice the radius. Check Z²/n² and n²/Z separately.
  15. Number of spectral lines to the ground state = n(n−1)/2. From n = 4 that is 6.
