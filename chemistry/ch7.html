CBSE Class 11 Chemistry · Chapter 7Redox Reactions

Complete chapter notes — oxidation numbers, every type of redox change, both balancing methods worked step by step, redox titrations and the electrode processes behind them.

The spine of this chapter: a redox reaction is nothing but electron bookkeeping. Assign oxidation numbers, see which element goes up (oxidation, electrons lost) and which goes down (reduction, electrons gained), and every question in the chapter — identify, classify, balance, titrate, predict — is answered from that one table of numbers. Oxidation and reduction always occur together and in exactly equal electron counts; that equality is the balancing method.

1 · Two Concepts of Oxidation and Reduction

Memory hook
OIL RIG — Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain (of electrons). And remember the agents are named for what they do to the other substance: the oxidising agent is itself reduced, the reducing agent is itself oxidised.
The electronic definition is the general one — it covers reactions with no oxygen at all. In  2Na(s) + Cl2(g) → 2NaCl(s), sodium loses electrons (oxidised, reducing agent) and chlorine gains them (reduced, oxidising agent).

2 · Oxidation Number

Oxidation number is the charge an atom appears to have in a species when all the bonds are treated as fully ionic — the shared electrons of each bond assigned entirely to the more electronegative atom.

2.1 The rules, in order of priority

  1. An atom in its free element has oxidation number 0: Na, O2, P4, S8, Cl2.
  2. A monatomic ion has an oxidation number equal to its charge: Na+ is $+1$, S2− is $-2$.
  3. Fluorine is always $-1$ — no exceptions, it is the most electronegative element.
  4. Group 1 metals are $+1$, group 2 are $+2$, aluminium is $+3$ in all their compounds.
  5. Hydrogen is $+1$ with non-metals, but $-1$ in metal hydrides (NaH, CaH2, LiAlH4).
  6. Oxygen is $-2$, except: $-1$ in peroxides (H2O2, Na2O2), $-\tfrac12$ in superoxides (KO2), and positive with fluorine ($+2$ in OF2, $+1$ in O2F2).
  7. The sum of the oxidation numbers equals 0 for a neutral molecule and equals the charge for an ion.
Worked example 1 — computing oxidation numbers

(a) Cr in Cr2O72−: $2x + 7(-2) = -2 \Rightarrow 2x = 12 \Rightarrow x =$ +6

(b) Mn in MnO4−: $x + 4(-2) = -1 \Rightarrow x =$ +7

(c) Fe in Fe3O4: $3x + 4(-2) = 0 \Rightarrow x =$ +8/3 — a fractional average, because Fe3O4 is really FeO·Fe2O3, containing one Fe(II) and two Fe(III).

(d) S in Na2S4O6 (sodium tetrathionate): $2(+1) + 4x + 6(-2) = 0 \Rightarrow 4x = 10 \Rightarrow x =$ +2.5 — again an average: two of the sulfurs are at $+5$ and two, joined by an S–S bond, are at $0$.

(e) C in C6H12O6: $6x + 12(+1) + 6(-2) = 0 \Rightarrow x =$ 0, even though the individual carbons are certainly not all zero.

Oxidation number is not valency
  1. Oxidation number may be positive, negative, zero or fractional; valency is always a whole number and has no sign. In CH4 the oxidation number of carbon is $-4$ while its valency is 4.
  2. A fractional value always means an average over atoms in genuinely different environments. Never write "the oxidation state of Fe is $+8/3$" without saying it is an average.
  3. Stock notation puts the oxidation number in Roman numerals after the element: Fe(III) in Fe2O3, Mn(VII) in KMnO4, Cu(I) in Cu2O.

Frequently asked
Why does fluorine not disproportionate? Because fluorine is the most electronegative element and has no positive oxidation state — it can only be reduced from 0 to $-1$, never oxidised. So F2 with alkali gives OF2 or O2 (oxidising the oxygen), not the F−/FO− pair that chlorine gives.

4 · Balancing Redox Reactions

4.1 The oxidation number method

METHOD 1  Balancing by the oxidation number method
  1. Write the skeletal equation and assign oxidation numbers to every atom.
  2. Identify the elements whose oxidation numbers change, and compute the increase and the decrease per atom, then per formula unit.
  3. Multiply the two species by whatever coefficients make the total increase equal to the total decrease.
  4. Balance all remaining atoms except H and O by inspection.
  5. Balance oxygen by adding H2O, then hydrogen by adding H+ (acidic medium).
  6. In a basic medium, finish by adding as many OH− ions to both sides as there are H+, and combine H+ + OH− into H2O.
  7. Verify that both the atoms and the total charge balance.
Step 3 is the whole method: electrons lost = electrons gained is the same statement as total increase = total decrease.
Worked example 2 — dichromate and iron(II) in acid

Skeleton:  Fe2+ + Cr2O72− + H+ → Fe3+ + Cr3+ + H2O

Changes: Fe goes $+2 \to +3$ — an increase of 1 per atom. Cr goes $+6 \to +3$ — a decrease of 3 per atom, and there are 2 Cr atoms, so 6 per dichromate ion.

Equalise: multiply Fe2+ by 6.   $6 \times 1 = 1 \times 6$ ✓

6Fe2+ + Cr2O72− → 6Fe3+ + 2Cr3+

Balance O with 7H2O on the right, then H with 14H+ on the left:

6Fe2+ + Cr2O72− + 14H+ → 6Fe3+ + 2Cr3+ + 7H2O

Charge check: left $= 6(+2) + (-2) + 14(+1) = +24$; right $= 6(+3) + 2(+3) = +24$. Balanced.

4.2 The half-reaction (ion–electron) method

METHOD 2  Half-reaction method — acidic medium
  1. Split the reaction into the oxidation half and the reduction half.
  2. In each half, balance all atoms except H and O.
  3. Balance O by adding H2O to the deficient side.
  4. Balance H by adding H+.
  5. Balance the charge by adding electrons — to the left of the reduction half, to the right of the oxidation half.
  6. Multiply the two halves so that the electrons cancel, then add them together.
  7. Cancel anything appearing on both sides, and check atoms and charge.
For a basic medium, do all of the above, then add OH− to both sides equal to the number of H+, convert H+ + OH− → H2O, and cancel duplicated water molecules.
Worked example 3 — permanganate and sulfite in acid

Skeleton:  MnO4− + SO32− → Mn2+ + SO42−  (acidic)

Reduction half (Mn: $+7 \to +2$):

MnO4− → Mn2+ + 4H2O  →  MnO4− + 8H+ → Mn2+ + 4H2O  →  MnO4− + 8H+ + 5e− → Mn2+ + 4H2O

Oxidation half (S: $+4 \to +6$):

SO32− + H2O → SO42− + 2H+  →  SO32− + H2O → SO42− + 2H+ + 2e−

Equalise electrons: reduction $\times 2$, oxidation $\times 5$ (LCM of 5 and 2 is 10), then add and cancel:

2MnO4− + 5SO32− + 6H+ → 2Mn2+ + 5SO42− + 3H2O

Check. O: $8+15 = 23$ left, $20+3 = 23$ right ✓. H: 6 both sides ✓. Charge: $-2-10+6 = -6$ left, $+4-10 = -6$ right ✓ Balanced.

Worked example 4 — permanganate and iodide in alkali

Skeleton:  MnO4− + I− → MnO2 + I2  (basic)

Reduction half (Mn: $+7 \to +4$), balanced first in acid then converted:

MnO4− + 4H+ + 3e− → MnO2 + 2H2O  →  add 4OH− to both sides  →  MnO4− + 2H2O + 3e− → MnO2 + 4OH−

Oxidation half:  2I− → I2 + 2e−

Multiply the first by 2 and the second by 3 (six electrons each), then add:

2MnO4− + 6I− + 4H2O → 2MnO2 + 3I2 + 8OH−

Charge check: left $= -2 - 6 = -8$; right $= -8$ ✓ Balanced.

Where balancing goes wrong
  1. Never add H+ to a basic medium in the final equation, and never add OH− to an acidic one.
  2. Electrons must cancel completely. If any remain in the final equation, the halves were not multiplied correctly.
  3. Check the total charge, not only the atoms. A charge mismatch is the fastest way to catch an error.
  4. Never change a formula (writing SO4− for SO42−) to make the balancing work — only coefficients may change.

5 · Redox Titrations

The end point of a redox titration is found either from a self-indicating reagent (KMnO4, whose first permanent pink tinge marks the end point) or from an external indicator (starch, giving a blue-black colour with iodine).

5.1 Permanganate — a different product in each medium

MediumHalf-reactionElectrons ($n$-factor)Mn goes
Acidic (dilute H2SO4)MnO4− + 8H+ + 5e− → Mn2+ + 4H2O5$+7 \to +2$ (colourless)
Neutral / faintly alkalineMnO4− + 2H2O + 3e− → MnO2 + 4OH−3$+7 \to +4$ (brown solid)
Strongly alkalineMnO4− + e− → MnO42−1$+7 \to +6$ (green)
Why sulfuric acid and never HCl or HNO3

Permanganate titrations are acidified with dilute H2SO4. Hydrochloric acid would itself be oxidised to chlorine, consuming extra KMnO4 and giving a high result; nitric acid is itself an oxidising agent and would attack the reducing agent being estimated.

5.2 Dichromate and iodometry

Dichromate works only in acid, with $n = 6$:  Cr2O72− + 14H+ + 6e− → 2Cr3+ + 7H2O. It is a primary standard — obtainable pure and stable — which KMnO4 is not.

Iodometry liberates iodine, which is then titrated against standard sodium thiosulfate with starch near the end point:

I2 + 2S2O32− → 2I− + S4O62−

Note the sulfur: $+2$ in thiosulfate rises to the average $+2.5$ in tetrathionate, so each thiosulfate loses exactly one electron.

5.3 Equivalents and $n$-factor

$n$-factor $=$ number of electrons gained or lost per formula unit.  Equivalent mass $= \dfrac{\text{molar mass}}{n\text{-factor}}$,  Normality $=$ Molarity $\times\ n$-factor, and at the end point  $N_1V_1 = N_2V_2$.
Worked example 5 — a titration calculation

25.0 mL of an FeSO4 solution needs 20.0 mL of 0.02 M KMnO4 in dilute H2SO4. Find the molarity of the iron(II) solution.

Reaction: MnO4− + 5Fe2+ + 8H+ → Mn2+ + 5Fe3+ + 4H2O, so the mole ratio is 1 : 5.

Moles of MnO4− $= 0.02 \times 0.0200 = 4.0\times10^{-4}$ mol  ⇒  moles of Fe2+ $= 5 \times 4.0\times10^{-4} = 2.0\times10^{-3}$ mol

$M(\text{Fe}^{2+}) = \dfrac{2.0\times10^{-3}}{0.0250} =$ 0.080 M

Same answer by equivalents: $N(\text{KMnO}_4) = 0.02\times5 = 0.1$ N; $N_1V_1 = N_2V_2 \Rightarrow N(\text{Fe}^{2+}) = \dfrac{0.1\times20}{25} = 0.08$ N, and since $n=1$ for Fe2+, $M = 0.08$ M.

6 · Redox Reactions and Electrode Processes

If the two halves of a redox reaction are physically separated, the electrons must travel through an external wire — and that current is usable work. This is a galvanic (voltaic) cell.

Cell notation: Zn(s) | Zn2+(1 M) ‖ Cu2+(1 M) | Cu(s) — anode written on the left, cathode on the right, the double line being the salt bridge.
EMF: $E^\circ_{\text{cell}} = E^\circ_{\text{cathode}} - E^\circ_{\text{anode}}$ (both as reduction potentials). A positive $E^\circ_{\text{cell}}$ means the reaction is spontaneous as written.

Electrode potentials are measured against the standard hydrogen electrode (SHE), defined as exactly 0.00 V: Pt | H2(1 bar) | H+(1 M) at 298 K.

Reading the electrochemical series. A more negative $E^\circ$ means a stronger reducing agent (more willing to be oxidised) — so Li, K, Ca, Na, Mg, Al, Zn, Fe are all good reducing agents. A more positive $E^\circ$ means a stronger oxidising agent — F2 ($+2.87$ V) is the strongest of all.
Worked example 6 — predicting whether a reaction happens

(a) Will zinc displace copper from CuSO4? $E^\circ_{\text{cell}} = +0.34 - (-0.76) = +1.10$ V, positive ⇒ yes, it happens spontaneously.

(b) Will copper displace zinc from ZnSO4? $E^\circ_{\text{cell}} = -0.76 - 0.34 = -1.10$ V ⇒ no.

(c) Will copper liberate hydrogen from dilute HCl? $E^\circ(\text{Cu}^{2+}/\text{Cu}) = +0.34$ V is above hydrogen's 0.00 V, so $E^\circ_{\text{cell}} = 0.00 - 0.34 = -0.34$ V ⇒ no — this is why copper vessels resist dilute acids while zinc does not.

Memory hook
Activity series, most reactive first: Li K Ba Ca Na Mg Al Zn Fe Ni Sn Pb (H) Cu Hg Ag Au — "Little Katie Bakes Cakes, Naughty Maggie Always Zaps Ferocious Nice Snakes, Pb… Here Curious Hungry Agents Audition." Any metal displaces every metal below it from solution.

7 · Where marks are lost

The twelve traps of Chapter 7
  1. The oxidising agent is the one that gets reduced. Write the oxidation numbers down before naming any agent.
  2. Oxidation number can be fractional; valency cannot. A fraction always means an average over inequivalent atoms.
  3. Oxygen is not always $-2$ — it is $-1$ in peroxides, $-\tfrac12$ in superoxides and positive in OF2. Fluorine, however, is always $-1$.
  4. Hydrogen is $-1$ in metal hydrides (NaH, CaH2).
  5. Disproportionation needs an intermediate oxidation state. Fluorine cannot disproportionate; nor can an element already at its highest or lowest state.
  6. Balance the charge as well as the atoms. An equation with balanced atoms and unbalanced charge is still wrong.
  7. In basic medium, finish with OH−, not H+. Add OH− to both sides and collapse the pairs into water.
  8. KMnO4 gives a different product in each medium — $n = 5$ in acid, 3 in neutral, 1 in strong alkali. Read the medium before writing the half-reaction.
  9. Acidify permanganate with dilute H2SO4 only, never HCl (oxidised to Cl2) or HNO3 (itself an oxidant).
  10. Not every reaction with oxygen is redox, and not every redox has oxygen. Precipitation and acid–base neutralisation involve no change in oxidation number at all.
  11. In a galvanic cell the anode is negative (the opposite of an electrolytic cell) and oxidation always occurs there.
  12. $E^\circ$ values are not multiplied by stoichiometric coefficients. Doubling a half-reaction does not double its electrode potential.
