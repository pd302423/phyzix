Matter is anything that has mass and occupies space. It is classified two ways — by physical state and by chemical composition.

2 · Handling Numbers

2.1 Precision and accuracy

Precision is how closely repeated measurements agree with one another; accuracy is how close a measurement is to the true value. A set of readings can be precise but inaccurate (a systematically mis-calibrated balance) — never assume one implies the other.

2.2 Significant figures

Significant figures are the digits known with certainty plus one final uncertain digit. They record how good a measurement is, so they must survive the arithmetic.

Memory hook
Addition → After the point (decimal places). Multiplication → count the Meaningful digits (significant figures).
Worked example 1 — significant figures in arithmetic

(a) $12.11 + 18.0 + 1.012 = 31.122 \Rightarrow$ 31.1 (18.0 has only one decimal place).
(b) $2.5 \times 1.25 = 3.125 \Rightarrow$ 3.1 (2.5 has only two significant figures).

Rounding off. If the digit to be dropped is greater than 5, raise the preceding digit by one; if less than 5, leave it; if it is exactly 5, raise the preceding digit only when it is odd (so 6.35 → 6.4, but 6.25 → 6.2).

2.3 Dimensional analysis (the factor-label method)

Multiply the given quantity by conversion factors written as fractions, arranged so that every unwanted unit cancels. The method never asks you to remember whether to multiply or divide — the units decide.

Worked example 2 — unit conversion

Convert 2.0 L to m3:   $2.0\ \text{L} \times \dfrac{10^{-3}\ \text{m}^3}{1\ \text{L}} =$ 2.0 $\times$ 10−3 m3.

Convert a density of 1.15 g cm−3 to kg m−3:  $\dfrac{1.15\ \text{g}}{\text{cm}^3}\times\dfrac{1\ \text{kg}}{10^3\ \text{g}}\times\dfrac{10^6\ \text{cm}^3}{1\ \text{m}^3} =$ 1.15 $\times$ 103 kg m−3.

3 · Laws of Chemical Combination

Five experimental laws were established before any atomic theory existed. Dalton's theory was invented afterwards, to explain them.

3.1 Law of conservation of mass (Lavoisier, 1789)

Matter can neither be created nor destroyed in a chemical reaction: total mass of reactants $=$ total mass of products.

This is why chemical equations must be balanced, and it is the licence for every stoichiometric calculation you will ever do.

3.2 Law of definite (constant) proportions (Proust, 1799)

A given compound always contains exactly the same elements in the same proportion by mass, whatever its source or method of preparation.

Pure water from a river, from combustion of hydrogen, or from a laboratory synthesis is always 1 : 8 hydrogen to oxygen by mass. This law is what makes a chemical formula meaningful at all.

3.3 Law of multiple proportions (Dalton, 1803)

If two elements combine to form more than one compound, the masses of one element that combine with a fixed mass of the other are in a simple whole-number ratio.
Worked example 3 — verifying the law of multiple proportions

Carbon and oxygen form CO and CO2. Fix the carbon at 12 g:

In CO, 12 g C combines with 16 g O.   In CO2, 12 g C combines with 32 g O.

Ratio of the oxygen masses $= 16 : 32 =$ 1 : 2, a simple whole-number ratio. The law is obeyed.

Same treatment for H2O and H2O2: fix 2 g of hydrogen; oxygen masses are 16 g and 32 g, again 1 : 2.

3.4 Gay-Lussac's law of gaseous volumes (1808)

When gases combine or are produced in a chemical reaction, they do so in a simple whole-number ratio by volume, provided all volumes are measured at the same temperature and pressure.

H2 + Cl2 → 2HCl combines in the volume ratio 1 : 1 : 2 — exactly the ratio of the coefficients.

3.5 Avogadro's law (1811)

Equal volumes of all gases, at the same temperature and pressure, contain equal numbers of molecules.

Avogadro's insight was that the particles of an element need not be single atoms: taking hydrogen and oxygen to be diatomic resolved the contradiction between Gay-Lussac's volume ratios and Dalton's atomic theory. Its immediate consequence is that for gases, volume ratio = mole ratio — the reason gaseous stoichiometry can skip masses entirely.

Frequently asked
"Which law explains why gaseous reaction volumes are in simple ratios?" — Gay-Lussac's law is the observation; Avogadro's law is the explanation. Mention both, in that order.

4 · Dalton's Atomic Theory (1808)

  1. Matter consists of indivisible atoms.
  2. All atoms of a given element are identical in mass and properties; atoms of different elements differ.
  3. Compounds form when atoms of different elements combine in fixed, simple whole-number ratios.
  4. Chemical reactions only rearrange atoms — atoms are neither created nor destroyed.

What it explained: postulate 4 gives the law of conservation of mass; postulate 3 gives the laws of definite and multiple proportions.

Limitations you must be able to state
  1. It could not explain the law of gaseous volumes (that needed Avogadro's molecules).
  2. Atoms are not indivisible — they contain electrons, protons and neutrons.
  3. Atoms of the same element are not always identical in mass — isotopes exist (and isobars show different elements can share a mass).
  4. It cannot explain why atoms bond at all, nor the existence of non-stoichiometric compounds.

5 · Atomic and Molecular Masses

5.1 The unified mass unit

1 u (unified mass unit, also called 1 dalton, Da) $= \tfrac{1}{12}$ of the mass of one atom of 12C $= 1.66056\times10^{-24}$ g.

On this scale the 12C atom is exactly 12 u by definition, hydrogen is 1.008 u and oxygen 16.00 u.

5.2 Average atomic mass

Because elements occur as mixtures of isotopes, the atomic mass in the periodic table is a weighted average over natural abundance:

$\text{Average atomic mass} = \sum \left(\text{fractional abundance} \times \text{isotopic mass}\right)$
Worked example 4 — average atomic mass of chlorine

Chlorine is 75.77% 35Cl (34.9689 u) and 24.23% 37Cl (36.9659 u).

$\bar{A} = (0.7577)(34.9689) + (0.2423)(36.9659) = 26.4959 + 8.9568 =$ 35.45 u

Notice the answer lies nearer 35 than 37 — the weighted average always leans towards the more abundant isotope. That single observation is enough to eliminate wrong options in an MCQ.

5.3 Molecular mass and formula mass

Molecular mass is the sum of the atomic masses of all atoms in a molecule: for CH4, $12.011 + 4(1.008) = 16.043$ u.

Formula mass is used for ionic compounds, which have no discrete molecules — NaCl is a continuous lattice, so we quote the formula mass $22.99 + 35.45 = 58.44$ u for the formula unit NaCl.

Where this loses marks
  1. Writing "molecular mass of NaCl". Ionic solids have a formula mass; there is no NaCl molecule.
  2. Atomic/molecular mass is in u (a mass of one particle); molar mass is in g mol−1 (a mass of one mole). They are numerically equal, which is a deliberate design of the scale — not a coincidence you may ignore in the units.

6 · The Mole Concept

One mole is the amount of substance containing exactly $6.022\times10^{23}$ elementary entities. $N_A = 6.022\times10^{23}\ \text{mol}^{-1}$ is the Avogadro constant.

The entity must always be named: one mole of O means $6.022\times10^{23}$ oxygen atoms; one mole of O2 means that many molecules, and therefore $2N_A$ atoms.

6.1 Molar mass

$n = \dfrac{m}{M}$  where $m$ is mass in g and $M$ is molar mass in g mol−1. Also $n = \dfrac{N}{N_A}$ and, for a gas at STP, $n = \dfrac{V}{22.7\ \text{L}}$.

6.2 Molar volume and the STP trap

22.7 L or 22.4 L?
  1. STP is now 273.15 K and 1 bar, at which the molar volume of an ideal gas is 22.7 L mol−1 (22.71098 L). This is the value the current NCERT text uses.
  2. The older 22.4 L mol−1 applies at 273.15 K and 1 atm. It is not wrong — it answers a different question.
  3. Read the pressure stated in the question. If it says 1 bar use 22.7; if it says 1 atm use 22.4; state which you used.
  4. Molar volume applies to gases only. There is no 22.7 L of liquid water.
Worked example 5 — the standard mole conversions

(a) Moles in 5.6 g of N2: $M = 28.0$ g mol−1, so $n = 5.6/28.0 =$ 0.20 mol.

(b) Molecules present: $N = nN_A = 0.20 \times 6.022\times10^{23} =$ $1.2\times10^{23}$ molecules.

(c) Atoms present: N2 is diatomic, so $2 \times 1.2\times10^{23} =$ $2.4\times10^{23}$ atoms.

(d) Volume at STP: $0.20 \times 22.7 =$ 4.54 L.

Worked example 6 — counting atoms inside a compound

How many oxygen atoms are in 9.8 g of H2SO4?

$M(\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4) = 2(1.008)+32.06+4(16.00) = 98.08 \approx 98$ g mol−1, so $n = 9.8/98 = 0.10$ mol.

Each formula unit carries 4 oxygen atoms $\Rightarrow$ moles of O atoms $= 0.40$ mol.

$N = 0.40 \times 6.022\times10^{23} =$ $2.41\times10^{23}$ oxygen atoms.

7 · Percentage Composition and Formulae

7.1 Mass percentage of an element

$\%\,\text{element} = \dfrac{\text{total mass of that element in one mole of compound}}{\text{molar mass of the compound}} \times 100$

For H2O: $\%\text{H} = \dfrac{2\times1.008}{18.02}\times100 = 11.19\%$ and $\%\text{O} = \dfrac{16.00}{18.02}\times100 = 88.79\%$.

7.2 Empirical and molecular formula

Empirical formulaMolecular formula
The simplest whole-number ratio of atomsThe actual number of atoms in one molecule
CH for benzene; HO for hydrogen peroxide; CH2O for glucoseC6H6; H2O2; C6H12O6
Molecular formula $= (\text{empirical formula})_n$  where  $n = \dfrac{\text{molar mass}}{\text{empirical formula mass}}$. If $n = 1$ the two formulae coincide (H2O, NH3, CO2).
METHOD 1  Percentage composition → molecular formula
  1. Take 100 g of the compound, so each percentage becomes a mass in grams.
  2. Divide each mass by the atomic mass of that element to get moles of atoms.
  3. Divide every result by the smallest of them — this gives the simplest ratio.
  4. If any ratio is still fractional, multiply all of them by the smallest integer that clears it (×2 for .5, ×3 for .33, ×4 for .25). Write the empirical formula.
  5. Compute the empirical formula mass, find $n = M/\text{EFM}$, and multiply every subscript by $n$.
The steps never change; only the arithmetic does. Step 4 is where most answers go wrong — never round 1.5 to 2.
Worked example 7 — the standard NCERT numerical

8 · Stoichiometry and the Limiting Reagent

A balanced equation is read as a mole statement, never a mass statement:

CH4(g) + 2O2(g) → CO2(g) + 2H2O(g)

reads as "1 mole of methane reacts with 2 moles of dioxygen" — and, since all four are gases, also "1 volume with 2 volumes" by Avogadro's law. It does not read as 1 g with 2 g.

8.1 The limiting reagent

The limiting reagent is the reactant that is completely consumed first; it fixes the maximum amount of product. Every other reactant is in excess and is left over.
METHOD 2  Identifying the limiting reagent — the division test
  1. Convert the given mass (or volume) of each reactant to moles.
  2. Divide each reactant's moles by its own stoichiometric coefficient in the balanced equation.
  3. The smallest quotient identifies the limiting reagent.
  4. Base all product calculations on the limiting reagent alone.
  5. Excess left over $=$ (moles supplied) $-$ (moles actually consumed, from the limiting reagent's ratio).
Never decide the limiting reagent by comparing raw masses, or even raw moles — only moles divided by coefficients are comparable.
Worked example 8 — limiting reagent in the Haber process

50.0 kg of N2 and 10.0 kg of H2 are mixed:  N2(g) + 3H2(g) → 2NH3(g). Find the mass of NH3 formed.

$n(\text{N}_2) = \dfrac{50.0\times10^3}{28.0} = 1786$ mol  →  $1786/1 = 1786$

$n(\text{H}_2) = \dfrac{10.0\times10^3}{2.016} = 4960$ mol  →  $4960/3 = 1653$  ← smaller, so H2 is limiting

$n(\text{NH}_3) = \tfrac23 \times 4960 = 3307$ mol;  $m = 3307 \times 17.03 = 5.63\times10^4$ g $=$ 56.3 kg of NH3

N2 consumed $= 4960/3 = 1653$ mol, so N2 left over $= 1786 - 1653 = 133$ mol $=$ 3.7 kg unreacted N2.

Note how badly the raw-mass instinct fails here: there is five times as much nitrogen by mass, and nitrogen is still the reactant in excess.

8.2 Percentage yield

$\%\ \text{yield} = \dfrac{\text{actual yield}}{\text{theoretical yield}} \times 100$, where the theoretical yield is what the limiting reagent predicts.

The one-line reason
Molarity is temperature-dependent because it is defined per unit volume, and volume expands on heating; molality is defined per unit mass of solvent, and mass does not change with temperature. Expect this as a one-mark question — and it is the reason molality is preferred in colligative-property work next year.
DERIVATION 1  Molarity from mass percentage and density
  1. Take exactly 1 L $=$ 1000 mL of solution. Its mass is $1000\,d$ g, where $d$ is the density in g mL−1.
  2. If the solution is $x\%$ by mass, the mass of solute in it is $\dfrac{x}{100}\times 1000\,d = 10\,x\,d$ g.
  3. Moles of solute $=\dfrac{10\,x\,d}{M_B}$, where $M_B$ is the molar mass of the solute.
  4. This is contained in exactly 1 L, so it is the molarity.
$M = \dfrac{10 \times (\text{mass }\%) \times d}{M_B}$  — with $d$ in g mL−1. The 10 comes from 1000 mL ÷ 100%.
DERIVATION 2  Converting molarity to molality
  1. Again take 1 L of solution: mass of solution $= 1000\,d$ g and moles of solute $= M$ (numerically, by definition of molarity).
  2. Mass of solute $= M \times M_B$ g.
  3. Mass of solvent $=$ mass of solution $-$ mass of solute $= (1000\,d - M M_B)$ g $= \dfrac{1000d - MM_B}{1000}$ kg.
  4. Molality $=$ moles of solute per kg of solvent $= \dfrac{M}{(1000d - MM_B)/1000}$.
$m = \dfrac{1000\,M}{1000\,d - M\,M_B}$.  For dilute aqueous solutions $M M_B \ll 1000d$ and $d \approx 1$, so $m \approx M$ — which is why the two are often confused.

9.1 Dilution

Adding solvent changes the volume but not the moles of solute:  $M_1V_1 = M_2V_2$. For mixing two solutions of the same solute: $M_{\text{final}} = \dfrac{M_1V_1 + M_2V_2}{V_1+V_2}$.
Worked example 9 — concentrated acid to molarity

Commercial concentrated HCl is 38% by mass with density 1.19 g mL−1. Find its molarity, and the volume needed to prepare 250 mL of 0.50 M HCl.

$M = \dfrac{10 \times 38 \times 1.19}{36.46} =$ 12.4 M

Dilution: $M_1V_1 = M_2V_2 \Rightarrow V_1 = \dfrac{0.50 \times 250}{12.4} =$ 10.1 mL of the concentrated acid, made up to 250 mL.

Worked example 10 — molality and mole fraction together

2.5 g of ethanoic acid (CH3COOH, $M = 60$ g mol−1) is dissolved in 75 g of benzene ($M = 78$ g mol−1). Find the molality and the mole fraction of the acid.

$n_B = 2.5/60 = 0.0417$ mol;  $n_A = 75/78 = 0.962$ mol.

$m = \dfrac{0.0417}{0.075\ \text{kg}} =$ 0.56 mol kg−1    $x_B = \dfrac{0.0417}{0.0417+0.962} =$ 0.0416

10 · Where marks are lost

The ten traps of Chapter 1
  1. Molar volume is 22.7 L at 1 bar, 22.4 L at 1 atm — and applies to gases only. Say which standard you are using.
  2. Molarity is temperature-dependent; molality is not. Molarity uses volume of solution, not of solvent.
  3. Never decide a limiting reagent from masses. Moles ÷ coefficient, then compare.
  4. Leading zeros are never significant; trailing zeros after a decimal point always are. 0.0500 has three significant figures.
  5. Addition follows decimal places, multiplication follows significant figures. Mixing the two rules is the commonest silent error.
  6. Do not round an empirical-formula ratio of 1.5 up to 2 — multiply every ratio by 2 instead.
  7. One mole of O2 contains $2N_A$ atoms but $N_A$ molecules. Always ask what the question is counting.
  8. Average atomic mass is weighted by abundance, never a plain mean of the isotopic masses.
  9. Ionic compounds have formula mass, not molecular mass.
  10. Mass of solution $=$ solute $+$ solvent. "75 g of benzene" is the solvent mass, so the solution weighs 77.5 g — a favourite one-mark deduction in molality problems.

1 · Sub-atomic Particles

1.1 Discovery of the electron — cathode rays

In a discharge tube at very low pressure (10−4 atm) and high voltage, rays stream from the cathode to the anode.

  • They travel in straight lines and cast sharp shadows.
  • They are deflected towards the positive plate in an electric field ⇒ they carry negative charge.
  • Their properties (charge-to-mass ratio) are independent of the gas and of the electrode material ⇒ the electron is a universal constituent of all matter.
Thomson (1897) measured the charge-to-mass ratio by balancing electric and magnetic deflections: $\dfrac{e}{m_e} = 1.758820\times10^{11}$ C kg−1.

Millikan's oil-drop experiment (1909) measured the charge itself, $e = 1.602\times10^{-19}$ C, and showed that charge is always an integral multiple of $e$ — charge is quantised. Combining the two gives $m_e = \dfrac{1.602\times10^{-19}}{1.759\times10^{11}} = 9.1094\times10^{-31}$ kg.

1.2 Protons and neutrons

Anode (canal) rays, observed with a perforated cathode, are positively charged. Unlike cathode rays, their charge-to-mass ratio depends on the gas taken, and it is largest for hydrogen — giving the lightest positive particle, the proton. Chadwick (1932) bombarded beryllium with α-particles and found an electrically neutral particle of nearly the proton's mass: the neutron.

2 · Atomic Models and Their Failures

2.1 Thomson's model (1898)

The atom as a uniform sphere of positive charge with electrons embedded in it — the "plum pudding" or "watermelon" model. It accounted for electrical neutrality and nothing else, and was demolished by α-scattering.

2.2 Rutherford's α-scattering experiment (1911)

A thin gold foil (~100 nm) was bombarded with fast-moving α-particles (He2+) from a radioactive source, detected on a fluorescent ZnS screen.

Rutherford's nuclear model. The positive charge and nearly all the mass are concentrated in a minute nucleus; electrons revolve around it at high speed in circular orbits; the electrostatic attraction supplies the centripetal force.
The two fatal drawbacks — learn both
  1. Stability. By classical electrodynamics an accelerating charged particle continuously radiates energy. An orbiting electron accelerates, so it should spiral into the nucleus within about 10−8 s. Matter would not exist.
  2. Spectra. A continuously spiralling electron would emit a continuous spectrum. Atoms actually give sharp line spectra.

It also said nothing about how the electrons are arranged, and so nothing about chemical behaviour.

3 · The Quantum Ideas That Rescued the Atom

3.1 Wave nature of electromagnetic radiation

$c = \nu\lambda$  with $c = 3.0\times10^8$ m s−1.  Wavenumber $\bar\nu = \dfrac{1}{\lambda}$ (cm−1), the number of waves per unit length.

Order of the electromagnetic spectrum by increasing frequency (decreasing wavelength): radio < microwave < infrared < visible < ultraviolet < X-rays < γ-rays. Visible light runs from about 400 nm (violet) to 750 nm (red).

3.2 Planck's quantum theory (1900)

Classical physics predicted that a black body would radiate ever more strongly at short wavelengths (the "ultraviolet catastrophe"); the observed curve instead peaks and falls. Planck resolved it by abandoning continuous energy:

Energy is emitted or absorbed only in discrete packets called quanta, of energy  $E = h\nu = \dfrac{hc}{\lambda}$, with $h = 6.626\times10^{-34}$ J s. Emitted energy is always an integral multiple, $nh\nu$.

For light, one quantum is called a photon.

Electrons are ejected from a metal surface when light of sufficiently high frequency falls on it. Four observations, none of which the wave theory can explain:

DERIVATION 1  Einstein's photoelectric equation
  1. A single photon of energy $h\nu$ is absorbed by a single electron.
  2. Part of that energy, the work function $\phi = h\nu_0$, is spent freeing the electron from the metal surface.
  3. Energy conservation gives the rest to the electron as kinetic energy:
  4. $h\nu = h\nu_0 + \tfrac12 m_e v^2$.
  5. If the electron is stopped by a retarding potential $V_0$, then $\tfrac12 m_e v^2 = eV_0$, so $eV_0 = h(\nu-\nu_0)$.
$\text{KE}_{\max} = h\nu - h\nu_0 = h(\nu - \nu_0)$. A graph of KE against $\nu$ is a straight line of slope $h$ with $x$-intercept $\nu_0$ — the same slope for every metal, since $h$ is universal.
Worked example 1 — photoelectric numerical

The work function of caesium is 1.9 eV. Find its threshold frequency and the kinetic energy of an electron ejected by light of wavelength 500 nm.

$\phi = 1.9 \times 1.602\times10^{-19} = 3.04\times10^{-19}$ J  $\Rightarrow$  $\nu_0 = \dfrac{\phi}{h} = \dfrac{3.04\times10^{-19}}{6.626\times10^{-34}} =$ $4.59\times10^{14}$ Hz

$E = \dfrac{hc}{\lambda} = \dfrac{(6.626\times10^{-34})(3\times10^{8})}{500\times10^{-9}} = 3.98\times10^{-19}$ J

$\text{KE} = 3.98\times10^{-19} - 3.04\times10^{-19} =$ $9.4\times10^{-20}$ J $= 0.59$ eV

4 · Bohr's Model of the Hydrogen Atom (1913)

4.1 Postulates

  1. The electron revolves around the nucleus in certain fixed circular orbits of definite energy, called stationary states or energy shells.
  2. While in a stationary state the electron does not radiate energy — this is a postulate, introduced precisely to defeat the classical objection.
  3. Energy is emitted or absorbed only when the electron jumps between stationary states, with $\Delta E = E_2 - E_1 = h\nu$ (Bohr's frequency rule).
  4. The angular momentum of the electron is quantised: $m v r = n\dfrac{h}{2\pi}$,  $n = 1, 2, 3, \ldots$
DERIVATION 2  Radius and energy of the $n$th Bohr orbit

Part A — radius.

  1. The electrostatic attraction between a nucleus of charge $+Ze$ and the electron supplies the centripetal force: $\dfrac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\dfrac{Ze^2}{r^2} = \dfrac{m v^2}{r}$   ⇒   $m v^2 = \dfrac{Ze^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r}$  …(i)
  2. Quantisation of angular momentum: $mvr = \dfrac{nh}{2\pi}$  ⇒  $v = \dfrac{nh}{2\pi m r}$  …(ii)
  3. Substitute (ii) into (i): $m\left(\dfrac{nh}{2\pi m r}\right)^2 = \dfrac{Ze^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r}$
  4. $\dfrac{n^2h^2}{4\pi^2 m r^2} = \dfrac{Ze^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r}$  ⇒  $r = \dfrac{n^2h^2\varepsilon_0}{\pi m Z e^2}$
$r_n = 52.9\,\dfrac{n^2}{Z}\ \text{pm} = 0.529\,\dfrac{n^2}{Z}\ \text{Å}$  — so $r \propto n^2$ and $r \propto 1/Z$.

Part B — energy.

  1. Kinetic energy: from (i), $\text{KE} = \tfrac12 m v^2 = \dfrac{Ze^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0 r}$.
  2. Potential energy of the pair: $\text{PE} = -\dfrac{Ze^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r}$ (negative, since the force is attractive).
  3. Total energy $E = \text{KE} + \text{PE} = \dfrac{Ze^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0 r} - \dfrac{Ze^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r} = -\dfrac{Ze^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0 r}$.
  4. Insert the radius from Part A: $E_n = -\dfrac{m Z^2 e^4}{8\varepsilon_0^2 h^2 n^2}$.
$E_n = -2.18\times10^{-18}\,\dfrac{Z^2}{n^2}\ \text{J} = -13.6\,\dfrac{Z^2}{n^2}\ \text{eV}$.  Note $\text{PE} = 2E$ and $\text{KE} = -E$ — so the total energy is negative, which is exactly what "bound" means.

Also worth carrying: $v_n = 2.188\times10^{6}\,\dfrac{Z}{n}$ m s−1, so $v \propto Z/n$.

DERIVATION 3  The Rydberg formula from Bohr's model
  1. An electron falls from level $n_2$ to level $n_1$, emitting one photon: $\Delta E = E_{n_2} - E_{n_1}$.
  2. $\Delta E = -2.18\times10^{-18}\left(\dfrac{1}{n_2^2} - \dfrac{1}{n_1^2}\right) = 2.18\times10^{-18}\left(\dfrac{1}{n_1^2} - \dfrac{1}{n_2^2}\right)$ J.
  3. By Bohr's frequency rule $\Delta E = h\nu$, so $\nu = \dfrac{\Delta E}{h} = 3.29\times10^{15}\left(\dfrac{1}{n_1^2}-\dfrac{1}{n_2^2}\right)$ Hz.
  4. Wavenumber $\bar\nu = \dfrac{\nu}{c} = \dfrac{\Delta E}{hc}$.
$\bar\nu = 109{,}677\left(\dfrac{1}{n_1^2}-\dfrac{1}{n_2^2}\right)$ cm−1 — Rydberg's empirical formula, now derived, with $R_H$ obtained from $m$, $e$, $h$ and $\varepsilon_0$ alone. This agreement is the reason Bohr's model was accepted.
Worked example 2 — the standard Bohr numericals

(a) Radius of the 3rd orbit of Li2+: $Z = 3$, $n = 3$:  $r = 52.9\times\dfrac{9}{3} =$ 158.7 pm.

(b) Ionisation energy of He+: removing the electron means $n_1 = 1 \to n_2 = \infty$, so IE $= 13.6 \times Z^2 = 13.6\times4 =$ 54.4 eV.

(c) Wavelength of the first Balmer line (Hα, $n_2=3 \to n_1=2$):
$\bar\nu = 109677\left(\tfrac14 - \tfrac19\right) = 109677 \times 0.1389 = 15{,}233$ cm−1  ⇒  $\lambda = 1/15233 = 6.56\times10^{-5}$ cm $=$ 656 nm (red).

(d) Number of spectral lines emitted when an electron drops from the $n$th level to the ground state $= \dfrac{n(n-1)}{2}$. From $n=4$: 6 lines.

Limitations of Bohr's model — a guaranteed question
  1. Works only for one-electron species: H, He+, Li2+, Be3+. It fails for helium onward because it ignores electron–electron repulsion.
  2. Cannot explain the fine structure of spectral lines revealed by better spectrometers.
  3. Cannot explain the splitting of lines in a magnetic field (Zeeman effect) or an electric field (Stark effect).
  4. Says nothing about the shapes of molecules or chemical bonding.
  5. It violates the Heisenberg uncertainty principle by assigning a definite radius and a definite momentum at once — a fixed "orbit" is not allowed.

5 · Towards the Quantum Mechanical Model

5.1 de Broglie's dual nature of matter (1924)

DERIVATION 4  The de Broglie relation
  1. For a photon, Planck gives $E = h\nu = \dfrac{hc}{\lambda}$, and Einstein's mass–energy relation gives $E = mc^2$.
  2. Equating: $mc^2 = \dfrac{hc}{\lambda}$  ⇒  $\lambda = \dfrac{h}{mc} = \dfrac{h}{p}$.
  3. de Broglie's leap: if radiation can behave as a particle, matter can behave as a wave. Replace $c$ by the particle's speed $v$.
$\lambda = \dfrac{h}{mv} = \dfrac{h}{p}$.  For an electron accelerated through $V$ volts, $\lambda = \dfrac{h}{\sqrt{2meV}} \approx \dfrac{12.27}{\sqrt{V}}$ Å.

Confirmed by Davisson and Germer (1927), who diffracted electrons from a nickel crystal — diffraction is a purely wave phenomenon.

Why we never see a cricket ball diffract
$\lambda$ is inversely proportional to mass. For a 100 g ball at 10 m s−1, $\lambda \approx 6.6\times10^{-34}$ m — smaller than any aperture in the universe, so no diffraction is observable. Wave character only matters for particles of very small mass.

Bohr's fourth postulate now stops being arbitrary: a stable orbit must contain a whole number of electron wavelengths, $2\pi r = n\lambda = n\dfrac{h}{mv}$, which rearranges to exactly $mvr = \dfrac{nh}{2\pi}$.

5.2 Heisenberg's uncertainty principle (1927)

It is impossible in principle to determine simultaneously the exact position and the exact momentum of an electron:  $\Delta x \cdot \Delta p \geq \dfrac{h}{4\pi}$,  equivalently $\Delta x \cdot \Delta v \geq \dfrac{h}{4\pi m}$.

This is not a limitation of instruments; it follows from the wave nature of matter. Its immediate casualty is the word "orbit": a precise path requires precise position and momentum together.

Worked example 3 — why an electron cannot reside in the nucleus

Confining an electron to a nucleus means $\Delta x \approx 10^{-14}$ m. Then

$\Delta v \geq \dfrac{h}{4\pi m \Delta x} = \dfrac{6.626\times10^{-34}}{4\pi (9.11\times10^{-31})(10^{-14})} \approx 5.8\times10^{9}$ m s−1

This exceeds the speed of light, which is impossible — so an electron cannot be confined inside the nucleus.

6 · The Quantum Mechanical Model of the Atom

Schrödinger's equation, $\hat H\psi = E\psi$, replaces orbits by wave functions. Solving it for the hydrogen atom yields the allowed energies $E$ and the wave functions $\psi$ automatically — the quantum numbers fall out of the mathematics rather than being assumed.

$\psi$ itself has no physical meaning; $|\psi|^2$ is the probability density — the probability of finding the electron in a unit volume at that point. An orbital is the region of space where that probability is high (conventionally the surface enclosing 90% of the probability).

6.2 Shapes of orbitals and nodes

s orbitals are spherically symmetric, and the probability never vanishes in any direction. p orbitals are dumbbell-shaped, with the two lobes along the $x$, $y$ or $z$ axis and a nodal plane through the nucleus — the probability of finding a p electron at the nucleus is exactly zero. Four of the five d orbitals ($d_{xy}$, $d_{yz}$, $d_{zx}$, $d_{x^2-y^2}$) are four-lobed clover shapes; $d_{z^2}$ has two lobes along $z$ plus a doughnut in the $xy$ plane.

Nodes are regions of zero probability.  Angular (planar) nodes $= l$  ·  Radial (spherical) nodes $= n - l - 1$  ·  Total nodes $= n - 1$.
Worked example 4 — counting nodes

For a 3p orbital: $n=3$, $l=1$. Angular nodes $= 1$; radial nodes $= 3-1-1 = 1$; total $= 2 = n-1$. ✓

For a 3d orbital: angular $=2$, radial $=3-2-1=0$, total $=2$. For a 4s: angular $=0$, radial $=3$, total $=3$.

6.3 Energies of orbitals

In hydrogen (one electron), energy depends on $n$ only: $\text{2s} = \text{2p}$, and $\text{3s}=\text{3p}=\text{3d}$. In multi-electron atoms, electron–electron repulsion splits them and the energy depends on both $n$ and $l$.

Two competing effects explain the splitting. Shielding: inner electrons screen the nucleus, so an outer electron feels a reduced effective nuclear charge $Z_{\text{eff}}$. Penetration: within a shell, an s electron spends more time close to the nucleus than p, which does more than d, which does more than f. Greater penetration means less shielding, larger $Z_{\text{eff}}$ and lower energy — hence s < p < d < f within a shell.

The $(n+l)$ rule. The orbital with the lower $(n+l)$ fills first; if two orbitals have the same $(n+l)$, the one with the lower $n$ fills first. Example: 4s $(4+0=4)$ fills before 3d $(3+2=5)$; 3d $(=5)$ fills before 4p $(=5)$ because 3d has the smaller $n$.

6.4 Rules for filling electrons

  1. Aufbau principle. Electrons occupy the lowest-energy orbital available first, in the $(n+l)$ order above.
  2. Pauli exclusion principle. No two electrons in an atom can have all four quantum numbers the same — equivalently, an orbital holds at most two electrons and they must have opposite spins.
  3. Hund's rule of maximum multiplicity. Pairing in the orbitals of a given subshell does not begin until each orbital of that subshell has one electron, and all the singly occupied orbitals have parallel spins.

6.5 Electronic configurations and the two famous exceptions

Configurations are written by subshell, e.g. O ($Z=8$): 1s2 2s2 2p4, or in core notation [He] 2s2 2p4.

Where this loses marks
  1. Electrons are removed from the outermost shell first, which means 4s before 3d even though 4s was filled first. Fe ($Z=26$) is [Ar]3d64s2, but Fe2+ is [Ar]3d6 — not [Ar]3d44s2.
  2. Configurations are written in shell order (3d before 4s) but filled in energy order (4s before 3d). Both conventions are accepted; be consistent.
  3. Hund's rule is about a subshell, not the whole shell. 2p3 is three parallel unpaired electrons, one per orbital.

7 · Where marks are lost

The ten traps of Chapter 2
  1. Photoelectric KE depends on frequency; photocurrent depends on intensity. No amount of intensity below $\nu_0$ ejects a single electron.
  2. Bohr's model applies only to one-electron species — H, He+, Li2+, Be3+. Using $-13.6Z^2/n^2$ for lithium atoms is wrong.
  3. A spectral series is named by the level the electron lands on. $n_1 = 2$ is always Balmer, whatever $n_2$ is.
  4. Energy is negative and rises towards zero as $n$ increases. "Higher energy" means less negative, and the gaps get smaller, never larger.
  5. Total nodes $= n-1$, of which $l$ are angular. A 3s orbital has 2 radial nodes, not 3.
  6. In hydrogen 2s and 2p are degenerate; in every other atom they are not. Questions state which atom deliberately.
  7. $m_l$ counts orientations, not electrons. A p subshell has $2l+1 = 3$ orbitals and therefore 6 electrons.
  8. Orbit vs orbital — an orbital is a probability region and holds at most 2 electrons; only a Bohr orbit holds $2n^2$.
  9. Cr and Cu are the exceptions; d4s2 and d9s2 do not occur. Quote symmetry and exchange energy as the reason.
  10. Isotopes differ in neutrons, isobars in protons. "Same mass number, different atomic number" is an isobar — reversing the two is a standard one-mark loss.

1.1 Mendeleev — merits and drawbacks

Merits. (i) He left gaps for undiscovered elements and predicted their properties with remarkable accuracy — eka-aluminium (gallium), eka-boron (scandium) and eka-silicon (germanium) were all found later and matched. (ii) He corrected wrong atomic masses (beryllium, indium, gold) on the strength of chemical behaviour. (iii) Noble gases, discovered much later, slotted in as a new group without disturbing the table.
Drawbacks you must be able to list
  1. Position of hydrogen was uncertain — it resembles both the alkali metals (1s1, forms H+) and the halogens (one electron short of a full shell, forms H−, diatomic).
  2. Anomalous pairs. To keep chemically similar elements together, he had to place a heavier element before a lighter one: Ar (39.9) before K (39.1), Co (58.9) before Ni (58.7), Te (127.6) before I (126.9).

  4. Chemically dissimilar elements were grouped together (alkali metals with coinage metals Cu, Ag, Au).

1.2 Moseley and the modern periodic law

Moseley (1913) found that the frequency of the characteristic X-rays emitted by an element obeys $\sqrt{\nu} = a(Z-b)$ — a clean relationship with the atomic number, not the atomic mass. Atomic number is the fundamental property, and the anomalous pairs disappear the moment the table is ordered by $Z$.

Modern periodic law: the physical and chemical properties of the elements are a periodic function of their atomic numbers.

3 · Periodic Trends in Physical Properties

3.1 Effective nuclear charge — the engine behind every trend

$Z_{\text{eff}} = Z - \sigma$, where $\sigma$ is the screening (shielding) constant due to the inner electrons. Across a period $Z$ rises by one each step while the added electron enters the same shell and screens poorly, so $Z_{\text{eff}}$ increases sharply. Down a group both $Z$ and the number of screening electrons rise, so $Z_{\text{eff}}$ stays nearly constant while the number of shells increases.

Screening efficiency falls in the order s > p > d > f, because a more penetrating orbital keeps the electron closer to the nucleus.

3.2 Atomic radius

An atom has no sharp boundary, so radius is always defined operationally:

  • Covalent radius — half the internuclear distance between two identical atoms bonded by a single covalent bond (Cl2: 198 pm apart ⇒ 99 pm).
  • van der Waals radius — half the distance between the nuclei of two non-bonded neighbouring atoms in the solid. Always larger than the covalent radius, because no orbital overlap is involved.
  • Metallic radius — half the internuclear distance between adjacent atoms in a metallic crystal.
Across a period (left → right): radius decreases — $Z_{\text{eff}}$ rises while the shell number stays the same. Down a group: radius increases — a new shell is added and shielding grows.

3.3 Ionic radius

Cation < parent atom — an electron (often the whole outer shell) is removed while the nuclear charge is unchanged, so the remaining electrons are pulled in harder. Anion > parent atom — added electrons increase repulsion with no increase in nuclear charge.

Na (186 pm) → Na+ (95 pm); Cl (99 pm) → Cl− (181 pm).

Isoelectronic species have the same number of electrons; among them, the radius falls as the nuclear charge rises:
N3− (171) > O2− (140) > F− (136) > Na+ (95) > Mg2+ (65) > Al3+ (50) pm — all with 10 electrons.
Memory hook
For an isoelectronic series, line the species up by charge from most negative to most positive — that order is already the order of decreasing size. More protons, same electrons, tighter grip.

3.4 Ionization enthalpy

$\Delta_i H_1$ is the energy required to remove the most loosely bound electron from an isolated gaseous atom in its ground state:  X(g) → X+(g) + e−. It is always positive (endothermic).

Factors: ionization enthalpy increases with larger $Z_{\text{eff}}$ and decreases with larger atomic size and greater shielding; stable half-filled and fully-filled configurations raise it sharply.

Across a period: increases (size falls, $Z_{\text{eff}}$ rises). Down a group: decreases (size rises, shielding rises). Highest in the period: the noble gas. Lowest: the alkali metal.
The two anomalies, stated properly.
Be > B (and Mg > Al): boron's outgoing electron comes from a 2p orbital, which is higher in energy and better shielded than beryllium's filled 2s2, so less energy is needed.
N > O (and P > S): nitrogen has the exactly half-filled 2p3 configuration, which is extra stable; oxygen's fourth 2p electron is paired, and the electron–electron repulsion within that orbital helps push it out.

3.5 Successive ionization enthalpies

$\Delta_i H_1 < \Delta_i H_2 < \Delta_i H_3 < \ldots$ always. Removing an electron from an already positive ion is harder, because the remaining electrons are held by the same nuclear charge.

A sudden jump in the sequence signals that a noble-gas core has been reached, and reveals the number of valence electrons. Magnesium's third ionization enthalpy is enormous compared with its second, confirming that Mg has exactly two valence electrons and that Mg2+ is where it stops.

The chlorine-beats-fluorine anomaly — asked every year

$\Delta_{eg}H$ of Cl is more negative than F, and S more negative than O, even though F and O are higher in the group. Reason: the 2p subshell of fluorine is unusually small and compact, so the incoming electron suffers strong inter-electronic repulsion from the electrons already crowded there. Chlorine's larger 3p subshell accommodates the extra electron with far less repulsion.

Also: the second electron gain enthalpy is always positive — forcing a second electron onto an already negative ion requires work. That is why O2− formation is endothermic overall and only survives in a lattice.

4 · Periodic Trends in Chemical Properties

4.1 Valence and oxidation state

For representative elements the valence equals the number of valence electrons, or eight minus that number, whichever is smaller. Across period 3 the highest oxidation state with oxygen rises 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7 (Na2O, MgO, Al2O3, SiO2, P4O10, SO3, Cl2O7), because each of those electrons can be used in bonding.

Amphoteric means reacting with both acids and bases: Al2O3 + 6HCl → 2AlCl3 + 3H2O, and Al2O3 + 2NaOH → 2NaAlO2 + H2O. Basic character falls and acidic character rises across a period; down a group the reverse happens (so, in group 15, N2O5 is strongly acidic while Bi2O3 is basic).

4.3 Anomalous behaviour of second-period elements

Three reasons, and the exam wants all three: (i) exceptionally small size; (ii) high electronegativity and high charge/radius ratio; (iii) absence of d orbitals in the valence shell, so the covalency cannot exceed four.

Consequence: nitrogen forms NF3 but never NF5, while phosphorus forms both PF3 and PF5 using its 3d orbitals. Oxygen's maximum covalency is 2, sulfur's is 6 (SF6).

4.4 Diagonal relationship

The first element of a group often resembles the second element of the next group, diagonally down-right: Li–Mg, Be–Al, B–Si.

Reason: moving right across a period decreases size and increases electronegativity, while moving down a group does the opposite. Along a diagonal the two effects nearly cancel, leaving similar size, charge/radius ratio and electronegativity. Hence LiCl and MgCl2 are both deliquescent and soluble in ethanol; Li and Mg both form nitrides directly with N2; BeO and Al2O3 are both amphoteric.

1 · The Kössel–Lewis Approach

Atoms combine to attain the stable octet of the nearest noble gas — by transferring electrons (ionic bond) or by sharing them (covalent bond). A Lewis symbol shows the valence electrons as dots around the symbol; the number of dots gives the valence directly.

1.1 Writing a Lewis structure

METHOD 1  Drawing any Lewis structure
  1. Count the total valence electrons. For an anion add one per unit of negative charge; for a cation subtract one per unit of positive charge.
  2. Pick the central atom — the least electronegative one (never hydrogen, which forms only one bond).
  3. Join each outer atom to the centre with a single bond (2 electrons each).
  4. Distribute the remaining electrons as lone pairs on the outer atoms first, to complete their octets.
  5. If the central atom is still short of an octet, convert lone pairs on the outer atoms into multiple bonds.
  6. Check with formal charges; the best structure has formal charges closest to zero, with any negative charge on the most electronegative atom.
For CO32−: $4 + 3(6) + 2 = 24$ valence electrons, C central, one C=O and two C–O−, giving three equivalent resonance structures.
Formal charge $= $ (valence electrons in the free atom) $-$ (lone-pair electrons) $-\tfrac12$ (bonding electrons). Formal charges are a bookkeeping device for choosing between structures — they are not real charges on the atoms.
Worked example 1 — formal charges in ozone

O3 is drawn as Ocentral with one double and one single bond.

Central O: $6 - 2 - \tfrac12(6) = +1$.   Doubly bonded terminal O: $6 - 4 - \tfrac12(4) = 0$.   Singly bonded terminal O: $6 - 6 - \tfrac12(2) = -1$.

Sum $= +1 + 0 - 1 =$ 0, matching the neutral molecule — always check this.

2 · The Ionic Bond

An ionic bond forms when one atom transfers electrons to another and the resulting oppositely charged ions are held by electrostatic attraction in a three-dimensional lattice.

Conditions that favour it: low ionization enthalpy of the metal, highly negative electron gain enthalpy of the non-metal, and — decisively — a high lattice enthalpy.

Lattice enthalpy is the energy required to completely separate one mole of a solid ionic compound into gaseous ions. For NaCl it is 788 kJ mol−1. It rises with higher ionic charges and smaller ionic radii (roughly $\propto q_1q_2/r$), which is why MgO (2+, 2−) melts far above NaCl.
Frequently asked
The formation of Na+ and Cl− from the atoms is endothermic overall ($+496 - 349 = +147$ kJ mol−1). NaCl is stable only because the lattice enthalpy released on assembling the crystal far outweighs that cost. Ionic bonding is driven by the lattice, not by the ion formation.

2.1 Fajans' rules — covalent character in an ionic bond

Covalent character increases with (i) small cation, (ii) large anion, (iii) high charge on either ion, and (iv) a cation with a pseudo-noble-gas (18-electron) configuration, such as Cu+, Ag+, Zn2+, which polarises better than an alkali-metal ion of the same size.

Hence LiCl is more covalent than NaCl; AlCl3 is more covalent than MgCl2; and among the lithium halides, LiI is the most covalent. A more covalent halide is more soluble in organic solvents and has a lower melting point.

3.1 Resonance

When a single Lewis structure cannot describe a molecule adequately, the real structure is a resonance hybrid of several canonical forms. The hybrid is lower in energy — more stable — than any contributing structure.

In O3, both oxygen–oxygen bonds are equal at 128 pm — between a single bond (148 pm) and a double bond (121 pm). In CO32− all three C–O bonds are identical, and in NO3− likewise, which no single Lewis structure can show.

The classic misconception
  1. The molecule does not flip between canonical forms, and it is not a mixture of them. It is a single structure that none of the drawings captures on its own.
  2. Canonical forms have no independent existence; only the hybrid is real.
  3. All canonical forms must have the same positions of nuclei — only electrons move. Moving an atom gives a different molecule, not a resonance form.

5 · Valence Bond Theory and Hybridisation

VBT explains a covalent bond as the overlap of two half-filled atomic orbitals with opposite spins. Greater overlap gives a stronger bond, and the bond forms along the direction of maximum overlap — which is where directional character, and therefore shape, comes from.

Hybridisation is the intermixing of atomic orbitals of nearly equal energy belonging to the same atom, to give an equal number of identical hybrid orbitals of equal energy and shape. Hybrid orbitals are better directed and overlap more effectively, so they form stronger bonds.

Conditions: orbitals hybridised must belong to the same atom or ion, have comparable energies, and — importantly — hybridisation does not require the orbitals to be half filled; filled and empty orbitals may take part.

6 · Molecular Orbital Theory

Atomic orbitals of the combining atoms merge into molecular orbitals that belong to the molecule as a whole. Two atomic orbitals combining by LCAO always give two molecular orbitals: one bonding (lower energy, additive combination, electron density between the nuclei) and one antibonding (higher energy, subtractive, with a node between the nuclei).

Conditions for combining: the atomic orbitals must have (i) comparable energies, (ii) the same symmetry about the molecular axis, and (iii) sufficient overlap.
Energy order.

7 · Hydrogen Bonding

A hydrogen bond is the electrostatic attraction between a hydrogen atom covalently bonded to a highly electronegative, small atom (F, O or N) and a lone pair on another such atom. Strength 10–40 kJ mol−1 — far weaker than a covalent bond (~400) but far stronger than ordinary van der Waals forces.
IntermolecularIntramolecular
BetweenTwo different moleculesTwo groups within the same molecule
EffectAssociates molecules ⇒ raises melting point, boiling point, viscosity and solubility in waterTies the molecule up internally ⇒ lowers boiling point and water solubility

The two answers this always demands

Why is ice less dense than water? In ice each oxygen is tetrahedrally hydrogen-bonded to four others, producing an open cage-like structure with large empty spaces. On melting, some of these hydrogen bonds break, the cage collapses and the molecules pack closer — so water at 273 K is denser than ice, and ice floats.

Why does o-nitrophenol boil lower than p-nitrophenol? The ortho isomer forms an intramolecular hydrogen bond (the groups are adjacent), so its molecules are not associated with one another and it is volatile. The para isomer can only form intermolecular hydrogen bonds, associating the molecules and raising the boiling point — and making it far more soluble in water.

The subtle ordering

Hydrogen bond strength follows electronegativity: HF > H2O > NH3. But boiling point follows H2O > HF > NH3, because each water molecule has two hydrogens to donate and two lone pairs to accept, so it forms about four hydrogen bonds, whereas HF has only one hydrogen to donate. Total hydrogen bonding beats individual strength.

2 · Oxidation Number

Oxidation number is the charge an atom appears to have in a species when all the bonds are treated as fully ionic — the shared electrons of each bond assigned entirely to the more electronegative atom.

2.1 The rules, in order of priority

  1. An atom in its free element has oxidation number 0: Na, O2, P4, S8, Cl2.
  2. A monatomic ion has an oxidation number equal to its charge: Na+ is $+1$, S2− is $-2$.
  3. Fluorine is always $-1$ — no exceptions, it is the most electronegative element.
  4. Group 1 metals are $+1$, group 2 are $+2$, aluminium is $+3$ in all their compounds.
  5. Hydrogen is $+1$ with non-metals, but $-1$ in metal hydrides (NaH, CaH2, LiAlH4).
  6. Oxygen is $-2$, except: $-1$ in peroxides (H2O2, Na2O2), $-\tfrac12$ in superoxides (KO2), and positive with fluorine ($+2$ in OF2, $+1$ in O2F2).
  7. The sum of the oxidation numbers equals 0 for a neutral molecule and equals the charge for an ion.
Worked example 1 — computing oxidation numbers

(a) Cr in Cr2O72−: $2x + 7(-2) = -2 \Rightarrow 2x = 12 \Rightarrow x =$ +6

(b) Mn in MnO4−: $x + 4(-2) = -1 \Rightarrow x =$ +7

(c) Fe in Fe3O4: $3x + 4(-2) = 0 \Rightarrow x =$ +8/3 — a fractional average, because Fe3O4 is really FeO·Fe2O3, containing one Fe(II) and two Fe(III).

(d) S in Na2S4O6 (sodium tetrathionate): $2(+1) + 4x + 6(-2) = 0 \Rightarrow 4x = 10 \Rightarrow x =$ +2.5 — again an average: two of the sulfurs are at $+5$ and two, joined by an S–S bond, are at $0$.

(e) C in C6H12O6: $6x + 12(+1) + 6(-2) = 0 \Rightarrow x =$ 0, even though the individual carbons are certainly not all zero.

Oxidation number is not valency
  1. Oxidation number may be positive, negative, zero or fractional; valency is always a whole number and has no sign. In CH4 the oxidation number of carbon is $-4$ while its valency is 4.
  2. A fractional value always means an average over atoms in genuinely different environments. Never write "the oxidation state of Fe is $+8/3$" without saying it is an average.
  3. Stock notation puts the oxidation number in Roman numerals after the element: Fe(III) in Fe2O3, Mn(VII) in KMnO4, Cu(I) in Cu2O.

4 · Balancing Redox Reactions

4.1 The oxidation number method

METHOD 1  Balancing by the oxidation number method
  1. Write the skeletal equation and assign oxidation numbers to every atom.
  2. Identify the elements whose oxidation numbers change, and compute the increase and the decrease per atom, then per formula unit.
  3. Multiply the two species by whatever coefficients make the total increase equal to the total decrease.
  4. Balance all remaining atoms except H and O by inspection.
  5. Balance oxygen by adding H2O, then hydrogen by adding H+ (acidic medium).
  6. In a basic medium, finish by adding as many OH− ions to both sides as there are H+, and combine H+ + OH− into H2O.
  7. Verify that both the atoms and the total charge balance.
Step 3 is the whole method: electrons lost = electrons gained is the same statement as total increase = total decrease.
Worked example 2 — dichromate and iron(II) in acid

Skeleton:  Fe2+ + Cr2O72− + H+ → Fe3+ + Cr3+ + H2O

Changes: Fe goes $+2 \to +3$ — an increase of 1 per atom. Cr goes $+6 \to +3$ — a decrease of 3 per atom, and there are 2 Cr atoms, so 6 per dichromate ion.

Equalise: multiply Fe2+ by 6.   $6 \times 1 = 1 \times 6$ ✓

6Fe2+ + Cr2O72− → 6Fe3+ + 2Cr3+

Balance O with 7H2O on the right, then H with 14H+ on the left:

6Fe2+ + Cr2O72− + 14H+ → 6Fe3+ + 2Cr3+ + 7H2O

Charge check: left $= 6(+2) + (-2) + 14(+1) = +24$; right $= 6(+3) + 2(+3) = +24$. Balanced.

4.2 The half-reaction (ion–electron) method

METHOD 2  Half-reaction method — acidic medium
  1. Split the reaction into the oxidation half and the reduction half.
  2. In each half, balance all atoms except H and O.
  3. Balance O by adding H2O to the deficient side.
  4. Balance H by adding H+.
  5. Balance the charge by adding electrons — to the left of the reduction half, to the right of the oxidation half.
  6. Multiply the two halves so that the electrons cancel, then add them together.
  7. Cancel anything appearing on both sides, and check atoms and charge.
For a basic medium, do all of the above, then add OH− to both sides equal to the number of H+, convert H+ + OH− → H2O, and cancel duplicated water molecules.
Worked example 3 — permanganate and sulfite in acid

Skeleton:  MnO4− + SO32− → Mn2+ + SO42−  (acidic)

Reduction half (Mn: $+7 \to +2$):

MnO4− → Mn2+ + 4H2O  →  MnO4− + 8H+ → Mn2+ + 4H2O  →  MnO4− + 8H+ + 5e− → Mn2+ + 4H2O

Oxidation half (S: $+4 \to +6$):

SO32− + H2O → SO42− + 2H+  →  SO32− + H2O → SO42− + 2H+ + 2e−

Equalise electrons: reduction $\times 2$, oxidation $\times 5$ (LCM of 5 and 2 is 10), then add and cancel:

2MnO4− + 5SO32− + 6H+ → 2Mn2+ + 5SO42− + 3H2O

Check. O: $8+15 = 23$ left, $20+3 = 23$ right ✓. H: 6 both sides ✓. Charge: $-2-10+6 = -6$ left, $+4-10 = -6$ right ✓ Balanced.

Worked example 4 — permanganate and iodide in alkali

Skeleton:  MnO4− + I− → MnO2 + I2  (basic)

Reduction half (Mn: $+7 \to +4$), balanced first in acid then converted:

MnO4− + 4H+ + 3e− → MnO2 + 2H2O  →  add 4OH− to both sides  →  MnO4− + 2H2O + 3e− → MnO2 + 4OH−

Oxidation half:  2I− → I2 + 2e−

Multiply the first by 2 and the second by 3 (six electrons each), then add:

2MnO4− + 6I− + 4H2O → 2MnO2 + 3I2 + 8OH−

Charge check: left $= -2 - 6 = -8$; right $= -8$ ✓ Balanced.

Where balancing goes wrong
  1. Never add H+ to a basic medium in the final equation, and never add OH− to an acidic one.
  2. Electrons must cancel completely. If any remain in the final equation, the halves were not multiplied correctly.
  3. Check the total charge, not only the atoms. A charge mismatch is the fastest way to catch an error.
  4. Never change a formula (writing SO4− for SO42−) to make the balancing work — only coefficients may change.

Permanganate titrations are acidified with dilute H2SO4. Hydrochloric acid would itself be oxidised to chlorine, consuming extra KMnO4 and giving a high result; nitric acid is itself an oxidising agent and would attack the reducing agent being estimated.

5.2 Dichromate and iodometry

Dichromate works only in acid, with $n = 6$:  Cr2O72− + 14H+ + 6e− → 2Cr3+ + 7H2O. It is a primary standard — obtainable pure and stable — which KMnO4 is not.

Iodometry liberates iodine, which is then titrated against standard sodium thiosulfate with starch near the end point:

I2 + 2S2O32− → 2I− + S4O62−

Note the sulfur: $+2$ in thiosulfate rises to the average $+2.5$ in tetrathionate, so each thiosulfate loses exactly one electron.

5.3 Equivalents and $n$-factor

$n$-factor $=$ number of electrons gained or lost per formula unit.  Equivalent mass $= \dfrac{\text{molar mass}}{n\text{-factor}}$,  Normality $=$ Molarity $\times\ n$-factor, and at the end point  $N_1V_1 = N_2V_2$.
Worked example 5 — a titration calculation

25.0 mL of an FeSO4 solution needs 20.0 mL of 0.02 M KMnO4 in dilute H2SO4. Find the molarity of the iron(II) solution.

Reaction: MnO4− + 5Fe2+ + 8H+ → Mn2+ + 5Fe3+ + 4H2O, so the mole ratio is 1 : 5.

Moles of MnO4− $= 0.02 \times 0.0200 = 4.0\times10^{-4}$ mol  ⇒  moles of Fe2+ $= 5 \times 4.0\times10^{-4} = 2.0\times10^{-3}$ mol

$M(\text{Fe}^{2+}) = \dfrac{2.0\times10^{-3}}{0.0250} =$ 0.080 M

Same answer by equivalents: $N(\text{KMnO}_4) = 0.02\times5 = 0.1$ N; $N_1V_1 = N_2V_2 \Rightarrow N(\text{Fe}^{2+}) = \dfrac{0.1\times20}{25} = 0.08$ N, and since $n=1$ for Fe2+, $M = 0.08$ M.

6 · Redox Reactions and Electrode Processes

If the two halves of a redox reaction are physically separated, the electrons must travel through an external wire — and that current is usable work. This is a galvanic (voltaic) cell.

Cell notation: Zn(s) | Zn2+(1 M) ‖ Cu2+(1 M) | Cu(s) — anode written on the left, cathode on the right, the double line being the salt bridge.
EMF: $E^\circ_{\text{cell}} = E^\circ_{\text{cathode}} - E^\circ_{\text{anode}}$ (both as reduction potentials). A positive $E^\circ_{\text{cell}}$ means the reaction is spontaneous as written.

Electrode potentials are measured against the standard hydrogen electrode (SHE), defined as exactly 0.00 V: Pt | H2(1 bar) | H+(1 M) at 298 K.

Reading the electrochemical series. A more negative $E^\circ$ means a stronger reducing agent (more willing to be oxidised) — so Li, K, Ca, Na, Mg, Al, Zn, Fe are all good reducing agents. A more positive $E^\circ$ means a stronger oxidising agent — F2 ($+2.87$ V) is the strongest of all.
Worked example 6 — predicting whether a reaction happens

(a) Will zinc displace copper from CuSO4? $E^\circ_{\text{cell}} = +0.34 - (-0.76) = +1.10$ V, positive ⇒ yes, it happens spontaneously.

(b) Will copper displace zinc from ZnSO4? $E^\circ_{\text{cell}} = -0.76 - 0.34 = -1.10$ V ⇒ no.

(c) Will copper liberate hydrogen from dilute HCl? $E^\circ(\text{Cu}^{2+}/\text{Cu}) = +0.34$ V is above hydrogen's 0.00 V, so $E^\circ_{\text{cell}} = 0.00 - 0.34 = -0.34$ V ⇒ no — this is why copper vessels resist dilute acids while zinc does not.

Memory hook
Activity series, most reactive first: Li K Ba Ca Na Mg Al Zn Fe Ni Sn Pb (H) Cu Hg Ag Au — "Little Katie Bakes Cakes, Naughty Maggie Always Zaps Ferocious Nice Snakes, Pb… Here Curious Hungry Agents Audition." Any metal displaces every metal below it from solution.

7 · Where marks are lost

The twelve traps of Chapter 7
  1. The oxidising agent is the one that gets reduced. Write the oxidation numbers down before naming any agent.
  2. Oxidation number can be fractional; valency cannot. A fraction always means an average over inequivalent atoms.
  3. Oxygen is not always $-2$ — it is $-1$ in peroxides, $-\tfrac12$ in superoxides and positive in OF2. Fluorine, however, is always $-1$.
  4. Hydrogen is $-1$ in metal hydrides (NaH, CaH2).
  5. Disproportionation needs an intermediate oxidation state. Fluorine cannot disproportionate; nor can an element already at its highest or lowest state.
  6. Balance the charge as well as the atoms. An equation with balanced atoms and unbalanced charge is still wrong.
  7. In basic medium, finish with OH−, not H+. Add OH− to both sides and collapse the pairs into water.
  8. KMnO4 gives a different product in each medium — $n = 5$ in acid, 3 in neutral, 1 in strong alkali. Read the medium before writing the half-reaction.
  9. Acidify permanganate with dilute H2SO4 only, never HCl (oxidised to Cl2) or HNO3 (itself an oxidant).
  10. Not every reaction with oxygen is redox, and not every redox has oxygen. Precipitation and acid–base neutralisation involve no change in oxidation number at all.
  11. In a galvanic cell the anode is negative (the opposite of an electrolytic cell) and oxidation always occurs there.
  12. $E^\circ$ values are not multiplied by stoichiometric coefficients. Doubling a half-reaction does not double its electrode potential.

Chapter 7 · Redox Reactions

Oxidation = loss of electrons = increase in oxidation number.  Reduction = gain of electrons = decrease in oxidation number.  OIL RIG.
The oxidising agent is itself reduced; the reducing agent is itself oxidised.

Oxidation number rules, in priority order

1. Free element $=$ 0  (Na, O2, P4, S8, Cl2)
2. Monatomic ion $=$ its charge  (Na+ $=+1$, S2− $=-2$)
3. Fluorine is always $-1$ — no exceptions
4. Group 1 $=+1$, group 2 $=+2$, Al $=+3$
5. Hydrogen $=+1$, but $-1$ in metal hydrides (NaH, CaH2, LiAlH4)
6. Oxygen $=-2$, except $-1$ in peroxides (H2O2), $-\tfrac12$ in superoxides (KO2), positive with F ($+2$ in OF2)
7. Sum $=0$ for a neutral molecule, $=$ the charge for an ion
Worked values: Cr in Cr2O72− $=+6$ · Mn in MnO4− $=+7$ · Fe in Fe3O4 $=+8/3$ (average) · S in S4O62− $=+2.5$ (average) · C in C6H12O6 $=0$.
A fractional oxidation number always means an average over inequivalent atoms. Oxidation number may be positive, negative, zero or fractional; valency is always a whole number with no sign.

Balancing — the two methods

METHOD 1  Oxidation number method
  1. Assign oxidation numbers; identify which elements change.
  2. Compute the increase and decrease per atom, then per formula unit.
  3. Multiply so that total increase $=$ total decrease.
  4. Balance all other atoms except H and O by inspection.
  5. Balance O with H2O, then H with H+.
  6. In basic medium, add OH− to both sides equal to the H+, and combine into H2O.
  7. Check both atoms and total charge.
METHOD 2  Half-reaction (ion–electron) method
  1. Split into the oxidation half and the reduction half.
  2. Balance all atoms except H and O.
  3. Balance O by adding H2O.
  4. Balance H by adding H+.
  5. Balance charge by adding electrons.
  6. Multiply so the electrons cancel, then add the halves.
  7. Basic medium: add OH− to both sides equal to the H+, convert H+ + OH− → H2O, cancel duplicated water.

positive ⇒ spontaneous
Cell notation: Zn(s) | Zn2+(1 M) ‖ Cu2+(1 M) | Cu(s) — anode left, cathode right, double line $=$ salt bridge.
Daniell cell: $E^\circ = 0.34 - (-0.76) = +1.10$ V. Anode is negative in a galvanic cell and is where oxidation occurs. Electrons flow anode → cathode through the wire; the salt bridge carries ions the other way to keep both solutions neutral.
SHE: Pt | H2(1 bar) | H+(1 M), defined as exactly 0.00 V.  More negative $E^\circ$ = stronger reducing agent; more positive $E^\circ$ = stronger oxidising agent (F2, $+2.87$ V, is the strongest).
Activity series (most reactive first): Li K Ba Ca Na Mg Al Zn Fe Ni Sn Pb (H) Cu Hg Ag Au. Any metal displaces every metal below it from solution. Copper cannot liberate H2 from dilute acid because its $E^\circ$ is positive.
