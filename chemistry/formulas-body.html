3 · Bohr's model — results and the derivation chain

Radius of nth orbit
$r_n = 52.9\,\dfrac{n^2}{Z}$ pm
$r \propto n^2$, $r \propto 1/Z$
Energy of nth orbit
$E_n = -13.6\,\dfrac{Z^2}{n^2}$ eV
$= -2.18\times10^{-18}\,\dfrac{Z^2}{n^2}$ J
Quantised angular momentum
$mvr = \dfrac{nh}{2\pi}$
Velocity in nth orbit †
$v_n = 2.188\times10^{6}\,\dfrac{Z}{n}$ m s−1
Frequency rule
$\Delta E = E_2 - E_1 = h\nu$
Energy partition †
$\text{PE} = 2E$  ·  $\text{KE} = -E$
DERIVATION CHAIN †  Bohr radius and energy, in the order you write them
  1. Coulomb attraction $=$ centripetal force:  $\dfrac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\dfrac{Ze^2}{r^2} = \dfrac{mv^2}{r}$  $\Rightarrow$  $mv^2 = \dfrac{Ze^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r}$  …(i)
  2. Quantisation:  $mvr = \dfrac{nh}{2\pi}$  $\Rightarrow$  $v = \dfrac{nh}{2\pi m r}$  …(ii)
  3. (ii) into (i):  $\dfrac{n^2h^2}{4\pi^2mr^2} = \dfrac{Ze^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r}$  $\Rightarrow$  $\boxed{r = \dfrac{n^2h^2\varepsilon_0}{\pi m Z e^2}}$
  4. $\text{KE} = \tfrac12 mv^2 = \dfrac{Ze^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0 r}$  ;  $\text{PE} = -\dfrac{Ze^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r}$
  5. $E = \text{KE} + \text{PE} = -\dfrac{Ze^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0 r}$  $\Rightarrow$  $\boxed{E_n = -\dfrac{mZ^2e^4}{8\varepsilon_0^2h^2n^2}}$
  6. Transition:  $\Delta E = 2.18\times10^{-18}\left(\dfrac{1}{n_1^2}-\dfrac{1}{n_2^2}\right)$ J  $\Rightarrow$  $\bar\nu = \dfrac{\Delta E}{hc} = 109677\left(\dfrac{1}{n_1^2}-\dfrac{1}{n_2^2}\right)$ cm−1

The anomalies — memorise these four lines
  1. Be > B and Mg > Al (ionization): the outgoing electron is from a higher-energy, better-shielded p orbital, versus breaking a filled s2.
  2. N > O and P > S (ionization): half-filled p3 is extra stable and symmetrical; the paired p electron in O suffers repulsion.
  3. Cl > F and S > O (electron gain, more negative): F and O are so small that the compact 2p subshell causes strong inter-electronic repulsion.
  4. Isoelectronic radius falls as nuclear charge rises: O2−(140) > F−(136) > Na+(95) > Mg2+(65) > Al3+(50) pm.

Resonance structures

Rules. Canonical forms differ only in the positions of electrons, never of nuclei. They have no independent existence — only the hybrid is real, and it is lower in energy than any single form. All bonds in the hybrid are equivalent: O3 both bonds 128 pm (between single 148 and double 121); CO32− and NO3− all bonds identical — the nitrate ion has exactly the same three-form pattern as the carbonate ion drawn above.

Chapter 7 · Redox Reactions

Oxidation = loss of electrons = increase in oxidation number.  Reduction = gain of electrons = decrease in oxidation number.  OIL RIG.
The oxidising agent is itself reduced; the reducing agent is itself oxidised.

Oxidation number rules, in priority order

1. Free element $=$ 0  (Na, O2, P4, S8, Cl2)
2. Monatomic ion $=$ its charge  (Na+ $=+1$, S2− $=-2$)
3. Fluorine is always $-1$ — no exceptions
4. Group 1 $=+1$, group 2 $=+2$, Al $=+3$
5. Hydrogen $=+1$, but $-1$ in metal hydrides (NaH, CaH2, LiAlH4)
6. Oxygen $=-2$, except $-1$ in peroxides (H2O2), $-\tfrac12$ in superoxides (KO2), positive with F ($+2$ in OF2)
7. Sum $=0$ for a neutral molecule, $=$ the charge for an ion
Worked values: Cr in Cr2O72− $=+6$ · Mn in MnO4− $=+7$ · Fe in Fe3O4 $=+8/3$ (average) · S in S4O62− $=+2.5$ (average) · C in C6H12O6 $=0$.
A fractional oxidation number always means an average over inequivalent atoms. Oxidation number may be positive, negative, zero or fractional; valency is always a whole number with no sign.

Balancing — the two methods

METHOD 1  Oxidation number method
  1. Assign oxidation numbers; identify which elements change.
  2. Compute the increase and decrease per atom, then per formula unit.
  3. Multiply so that total increase $=$ total decrease.
  4. Balance all other atoms except H and O by inspection.
  5. Balance O with H2O, then H with H+.
  6. In basic medium, add OH− to both sides equal to the H+, and combine into H2O.
  7. Check both atoms and total charge.
METHOD 2  Half-reaction (ion–electron) method
  1. Split into the oxidation half and the reduction half.
  2. Balance all atoms except H and O.
  3. Balance O by adding H2O.
  4. Balance H by adding H+.
  5. Balance charge by adding electrons.
  6. Multiply so the electrons cancel, then add the halves.
  7. Basic medium: add OH− to both sides equal to the H+, convert H+ + OH− → H2O, cancel duplicated water.
Worked: 6Fe2+ + Cr2O72− + 14H+ → 6Fe3+ + 2Cr3+ + 7H2O  (charge $+24 = +24$ ✓)
2MnO4− + 5SO32− + 6H+ → 2Mn2+ + 5SO42− + 3H2O  (charge $-6 = -6$ ✓)
2MnO4− + 6I− + 4H2O → 2MnO2 + 3I2 + 8OH−  (basic; charge $-8 = -8$ ✓)

Titration arithmetic and electrode processes

Equivalent mass
$E = \dfrac{\text{molar mass}}{n\text{-factor}}$
Normality †
$N = M \times n\text{-factor}$
At the end point †
$N_1V_1 = N_2V_2$
Cell EMF
$E^\circ_{\text{cell}} = E^\circ_{\text{cathode}} - E^\circ_{\text{anode}}$
positive ⇒ spontaneous
Cell notation: Zn(s) | Zn2+(1 M) ‖ Cu2+(1 M) | Cu(s) — anode left, cathode right, double line $=$ salt bridge.
Daniell cell: $E^\circ = 0.34 - (-0.76) = +1.10$ V. Anode is negative in a galvanic cell and is where oxidation occurs. Electrons flow anode → cathode through the wire; the salt bridge carries ions the other way to keep both solutions neutral.
SHE: Pt | H2(1 bar) | H+(1 M), defined as exactly 0.00 V.  More negative $E^\circ$ = stronger reducing agent; more positive $E^\circ$ = stronger oxidising agent (F2, $+2.87$ V, is the strongest).
Activity series (most reactive first): Li K Ba Ca Na Mg Al Zn Fe Ni Sn Pb (H) Cu Hg Ag Au. Any metal displaces every metal below it from solution. Copper cannot liberate H2 from dilute acid because its $E^\circ$ is positive.
$E^\circ$ values are never multiplied by stoichiometric coefficients.
