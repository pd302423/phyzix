Chapter 1  ·  Some Basic Concepts of Chemistry
The mole — a counting unit

1 mole = 6.022×1023 particles. Defined as the number of atoms in exactly 12 g of carbon–12. Molar mass (g/mol) is numerically equal to atomic/molecular mass in u.

$n=\dfrac{m}{M}=\dfrac{N}{N_A}=\dfrac{V}{22.7\,\text{L}}=M\times V_{(L)}$

Molar volume: 22.71 L at STP (273.15 K, 1 bar) · 22.4 L at 273.15 K, 1 atm.

Avogadro's law: equal volumes of any gases at the same T and P hold equal numbers of molecules. So one mole of every gas takes the same volume — H2 and SO2 alike. The rationalised NCERT uses 1 bar → 22.71 L. Only use 22.4 L when the question says 1 atm.

Formulae, significant figures, limiting reagent

$\text{Molecular} = n\times\text{Empirical},\quad n=\dfrac{\text{Molar mass}}{\text{Empirical formula mass}}$

Benzene: EF = CH (13), M = 78 → n = 6 → MF = C6H6.

Sig figs: non-zero digits count · zeros between digits count · leading zeros do not · trailing zeros after a decimal do. + and − → fewest decimal places; × and ÷ → fewest significant figures.

Limiting reagent: divide moles ÷ stoichiometric coefficient for each reactant — the smallest value is limiting.

Never compare raw masses to find the limiting reagent. Always convert to moles first, then divide by the coefficient.
Chapter 2  ·  Structure of Atom
Cathode rays → the electron

Low-pressure gas, high voltage. Rays stream from the cathode (−).

  • Travel in straight lines
  • Deflected towards the positive plate → carry negative charge
  • Properties are independent of the gas and of the electrode material
If swapping hydrogen for neon for argon changes nothing, the particle cannot belong to any one gas — it must be a universal constituent of all matter. That is exactly how Thomson argued the electron is in every atom.

Thomson measured only the ratio by balancing electric against magnetic deflection:

$e/m = 1.758820\times10^{11}\ \text{C kg}^{-1}$

Deflection depends on (i) magnitude of charge, (ii) mass of the particle, (iii) strength of the field.

Thomson could not find e or m separately — only e/m.
Anode rays — why e/m varies

Cathode-ray electrons knock electrons off the gas atoms, creating positive ions that stream to the cathode.

Cathode raysAnode rays
IdentityElectronsPositive ions of the gas
MassAlways 9.11×10−31 kgDepends on the gas
ChargeAlways −1.6×10−19 C+1, +2 … depends how many e− stripped
e/mConstantVaries with the gas

Lightest positive particle = H+ = proton, 1.676×10−27 kg — and therefore the largest e/m of any anode ray.

Millikan's oil drop → the charge

Oil droplets are charged by X-ray-ionised air. The field is tuned until a droplet hangs motionless — electric force up exactly balances gravity down — which gives the droplet's charge.

Every droplet's charge came out an integral multiple of 1.602×10−19 C, never a fraction. That proves charge is quantised and this is the fundamental unit.

$m_e=\dfrac{e}{(e/m)}=\dfrac{1.602\times10^{-19}}{1.758\times10^{11}}=9.1094\times10^{-31}\ \text{kg}$

Thomson gave the ratio → Millikan gave the charge → together they gave the mass. That chain is a 3-marker.

Nucleus ~10−15 m, atom ~10−10 m → nucleus is 105 times smaller.

Two drawbacks: (1) an accelerating electron must continuously radiate energy, spiral in and collapse in ~10−8 s — but atoms are stable; (2) it predicts a continuous spectrum, whereas atoms give a line spectrum. It also says nothing about electron arrangement.

Bohr's model — the four postulates
  1. The electron revolves in certain fixed circular orbits called stationary states.
  2. The energy of an electron in a stationary state does not change with time; energy is emitted or absorbed only on jumping between orbits.
  3. Angular momentum is quantised: $mvr=n\dfrac{h}{2\pi}$
  4. Bohr's frequency rule: $\Delta E=E_2-E_1=h\nu$

$r_n=52.9\dfrac{n^2}{Z}\ \text{pm}\qquad E_n=-13.6\dfrac{Z^2}{n^2}\ \text{eV}=-2.18\times10^{-18}\dfrac{Z^2}{n^2}\ \text{J}$

Why is the energy negative? We define E = 0 for an electron infinitely far away and free. A bound electron is more stable than a free one, so it must have less energy than zero → negative. More negative = more tightly bound. n = 1 is the most negative = ground state.
Limitations of Bohr's model
  • Fails for any atom with more than one electron — it ignores electron–electron repulsion
  • Cannot explain the fine structure of spectral lines
  • Cannot explain the Zeeman effect (magnetic field) or the Stark effect (electric field)
  • Cannot explain chemical bonding or molecular shapes
  • Violates Heisenberg's uncertainty principle — a fixed orbit means exact position and exact momentum at once
  • Ignores the wave nature of the electron

Works only for one-electron species: H, He+, Li2+, Be3+.

Light arrives as photons of energy E = hν. One photon hits one electron and dumps all its energy at once. That single-packet picture explains the threshold, the instant emission, and why intensity only changes the count.
Dual nature, uncertainty, orbital

de Broglie: $\lambda=\dfrac{h}{mv}=\dfrac{h}{p}$  ·  Heisenberg: $\Delta x\cdot\Delta p\ \ge\ \dfrac{h}{4\pi}$

A cricket ball has enormous m, so λ ~ 10−34 m — unmeasurable. An electron's m is tiny, so λ is atom-sized and its wave nature dominates.

Orbit = a fixed circular path (Bohr — wrong). Orbital = a three-dimensional region where the probability of finding the electron is maximum (~90%).

ψ is the wave function — no physical meaning on its own. ψ2 is the probability density — the chance of finding the electron at that point. That is why orbitals are drawn as clouds.

They are n, l, ml, ms — not m1 and m2. ml is orientation; ms is spin. Check validity with l ≤ n−1 and |ml| ≤ l.

Nodes: radial = n − l − 1 · angular = l · total = n − 1. 3p → radial 1, angular 1, total 2.

Filling rules — Aufbau, Pauli, Hund

Aufbau: orbitals fill in order of increasing energy, given by the (n + l) rule — lower (n+l) first; if tied, lower n first. 4s (4+0=4) fills before 3d (3+2=5).

Pauli exclusion: no two electrons in an atom have all four quantum numbers the same → an orbital holds at most 2 electrons, with opposite spins.

Hund's rule of maximum multiplicity: degenerate orbitals are filled singly first, all with parallel spin, before any pairing begins.

Hund's rule exists because electrons repel — they stay in separate orbitals as long as there is room.

Reason: exactly half-filled and completely filled subshells are extra stable, because of (a) symmetrical distribution of electrons and (b) greater exchange energy.

Chapter 3  ·  Classification of Elements and Periodicity
The engine behind every trend

Every single trend in this chapter is a tug-of-war between three things:

  1. Effective nuclear charge (Zeff) — the net pull the outer electron actually feels. Pulls inward.
  2. Shielding / screening — inner electrons blocking that pull. Pushes outward.
  3. Number of shells — more shells, bigger atom.

Learn this one paragraph and you can derive every trend below instead of memorising five of them.

Periodic law, periods, blocks

Atomic and ionic radius

Across a period → DECREASES. Protons are added one at a time but the electrons go into the same shell, so shielding barely changes. Zeff rises → the shell is pulled in tighter.

Down a group → INCREASES. A whole new shell is added; the extra shielding outweighs the extra nuclear charge.

Cation < parent atom — electrons lost (often a whole shell), the same nuclear charge now holds fewer electrons.

Anion > parent atom — electrons added, more mutual repulsion, the same nuclear charge spread thinner.

Isoelectronic species (same electron count): radius falls as Z rises.

$\text{N}^{3-}>\text{O}^{2-}>\text{F}^->\text{Ne}>\text{Na}^+>\text{Mg}^{2+}>\text{Al}^{3+}$

All have 10 electrons; Z runs 7→13. More protons, same electrons → smaller.

Ionisation enthalpy — and both anomalies

Energy required to remove the most loosely bound electron from an isolated gaseous atom in its ground state. Always positive (endothermic).

Increases across a period · decreases down a group.

Why IE2 > IE1 always: after the first electron leaves, the species is a cation — the same protons now hold fewer electrons, so every one left is gripped harder.

AnomalyReason
Be (899) > B (801)In Be the electron leaves a 2s orbital. In B it leaves a 2p orbital — higher in energy and shielded by the 2s pair → easier to remove.
N (1402) > O (1314)N is 2p3 — exactly half-filled, extra stable, every electron in its own orbital. O is 2p4 — one orbital now holds a pair, and their mutual repulsion makes one easier to pull out.

Same logic repeats: Mg > Al and P > S.

Electron gain enthalpy — in full

$X(g)+e^-\longrightarrow X^-(g)\qquad \Delta_{eg}H$

The atom must be gaseous (isolated, no neighbours), neutral and in its ground state.

Sign convention: energy released → ΔegH is negative (the atom wants the electron). Energy must be supplied → ΔegH is positive (the atom resists it).

SpeciesSignWhy
HalogensMost negativeOne electron short of a full octet
Noble gasesPositiveOctet already complete — the electron must be forced into a new shell
Be, Mg (group 2)PositiveStable filled ns2 subshell
N~zeroStable half-filled 2p3

Trends: across a period → more negative (smaller atom, higher Zeff, keener to accept). Down a group → less negative (bigger atom, the incoming electron sits further from the nucleus).

The anomaly: Cl (−349) is more negative than F (−328) — and S more than O. F and O are second-period atoms: very small, with compact 2p subshells. Forcing an extra electron into that crowded little region causes strong inter-electronic repulsion, which cancels much of the energy released. Cl and S are third-period — roomier, less repulsion, so more energy is actually given out.

Electron affinity vs ΔegH: electron affinity is the energy released (positive when released); ΔegH is the enthalpy change (negative when released). Opposite signs for the same event. NCERT uses ΔegH.

Electronegativity, metallic character, oxides

Electronegativity: the tendency of an atom in a chemical compound to attract the shared pair of electrons. Cannot be measured directly — relative scale, no units. Pauling: F = 4.0 highest. Increases across a period, decreases down a group.

Electronegativity applies to an atom inside a bond; ΔegH applies to an isolated gaseous atom and is a measurable energy. Do not swap them.

Metallic character tracks how easily an atom loses electrons → inversely follows IE. Decreases across a period, increases down a group.

Oxides across period 3: Na2O, MgO basic → Al2O3 amphoteric → SiO2, P4O10, SO3, Cl2O7 acidic. Amphoteric = reacts with both acids and bases.

Diagonal relationship: Li–Mg, Be–Al, B–Si — the first element of a group resembles the second element of the next group, because they end up with similar size and polarising power.

Overlapping of atomic orbitals

A covalent bond forms when two half-filled atomic orbitals overlap. The greater the overlap, the stronger the bond.

σ bond — head-on / axial / end-to-end overlap along the internuclear axis:

  • s–s → H2
  • s–p → HF, HCl
  • p–p (axial, e.g. pz–pz) → F2, Cl2
  • hybrid–s or hybrid–hybrid → CH4, C2H6

Molecular orbital theory — the bonding / antibonding relationship

Two atomic orbitals combine by LCAO (linear combination of atomic orbitals). Two AOs in → always exactly two MOs out. The total number of MOs always equals the total number of AOs combined.

Bonding MO  (σ, π)Antibonding MO  (σ*, π*)
Formed byConstructive interference — waves in the same phase addDestructive interference — waves in opposite phase subtract
Wave function$\psi_b=\psi_A+\psi_B$$\psi_a=\psi_A-\psi_B$
Electron density between the nucleiConcentrated thereZero — there is a NODE between the nuclei
Effect on the nucleiThe shared cloud screens the two nuclei from each other and pulls both inwardThe nuclei are exposed to each other and repel
Energy vs the parent AOsLOWER → stabilisingHIGHER → destabilising
FilledFirst (Aufbau still applies)Only after the bonding MO is full

Five relationships to state in an answer:

  1. They are always produced as a pair — one bonding and one antibonding from every pair of combining AOs.
  2. The antibonding MO is raised more than the bonding MO is lowered. This is exactly why He2 does not exist: 2 electrons in σ1s and 2 in σ*1s give bond order 0, and the net effect is slightly destabilising.
  3. Each pair of electrons in an antibonding MO cancels one pair in the corresponding bonding MO.
  4. Conditions for two AOs to combine: comparable energy, same symmetry about the molecular axis, and sufficient overlap.
  5. Bond order $=\dfrac{N_b-N_a}{2}$. BO > 0 → the molecule exists. BO = 0 → it does not.

Energy order — and there are two of them. For B2, C2, N2 (14 electrons or fewer):

$\sigma 1s<\sigma^*1s<\sigma 2s<\sigma^*2s<\underline{\pi 2p_x=\pi 2p_y}<\sigma 2p_z<\pi^*2p_x=\pi^*2p_y<\sigma^*2p_z$

For O2, F2 (more than 14 electrons) the σ2pz drops below the π orbitals:

$\sigma 1s<\sigma^*1s<\sigma 2s<\sigma^*2s<\underline{\sigma 2p_z}<\pi 2p_x=\pi 2p_y<\pi^*2p_x=\pi^*2p_y<\sigma^*2p_z$

N2: $KK\ \sigma2s^2\ \sigma^*2s^2\ \pi2p_x^2\ \pi2p_y^2\ \sigma2p_z^2$  →  BO $=\frac{10-4}{2}=3$, all paired → diamagnetic

O2: $KK\ \sigma2s^2\ \sigma^*2s^2\ \sigma2p_z^2\ \pi2p_x^2\ \pi2p_y^2\ \pi^*2p_x^1\ \pi^*2p_y^1$  →  BO $=\frac{10-6}{2}=2$, two unpaired → PARAMAGNETIC

Magnetic behaviour: any unpaired electron in any MO → paramagnetic (attracted by a magnetic field). All paired → diamagnetic (weakly repelled). MOT is the only theory that predicts O2's paramagnetism — that is its headline success.

Lewis structures and formal charge
  1. Count total valence electrons (add for a negative charge, subtract for a positive one)
  2. Put the least electronegative atom in the centre — H is never central
  3. Join each outer atom with a single bond
  4. Complete the octets of the outer atoms first
  5. Leftover electrons → lone pairs on the central atom
  6. Still short → convert lone pairs into double or triple bonds

Valence electrons: H 1 · Be 2 · B 3 · C 4 · N 5 · O 6 · F/Cl 7 · P 5 · S 6 · Xe 8

$\text{FC}=V-L-\tfrac12 B$

V = valence electrons in the free atom, L = lone-pair electrons, B = bonding electrons. The best Lewis structure is the one with the smallest formal charges.

  • It says nothing about the shape of a molecule
  • It says nothing about the energy or relative stability of a molecule

Resonance

Definition: when a single Lewis structure cannot describe a molecule accurately, several structures with similar energy, the same positions of nuclei, and the same number of bonding and non-bonding electron pairs are taken as the canonical structures, and the molecule is described by their resonance hybrid.

MoleculeCanonical formsEvidence
O32Both O–O bonds equal at 128 pm — between single (148) and double (121)
CO32−3All three C–O bonds identical
CO23Both C–O bonds identical

The hybrid is lower in energy (more stable) than any canonical form — the difference is the resonance stabilisation energy.

The four things NCERT says resonance is NOT (a favourite question): canonical forms have no real existence; the molecule does not spend part of its time in each form; there is no equilibrium between them; the molecule has one single structure which simply cannot be drawn with one Lewis diagram.

Hybridisation — the three steps and the shortcut

1 Promotion — an electron is promoted to an empty orbital of the same shell to create enough half-filled orbitals. 2 Mixing — orbitals of nearly equal energy mix to give an equal number of identical hybrid orbitals. 3 Overlap — each hybrid overlaps head-on with another orbital → σ bond.

Dipole moment — polar vs non-polar

$\mu=Q\times r$  ·  unit debye (D), $1\ \text{D}=3.33564\times10^{-30}\ \text{C m}$

μ is a VECTOR — drawn as a crossed arrow pointing from positive to negative. For a molecule, μ is the vector sum of all bond moments.

$\mu=0\ \Rightarrow\ \textbf{non-polar molecule}\qquad \mu\ne0\ \Rightarrow\ \textbf{polar molecule}$

A molecule can have polar bonds and still be non-polar. If the geometry is symmetric, the bond moments cancel as vectors. Rule of thumb: no lone pairs on the central atom AND all surrounding atoms identical → non-polar. Lone pairs or mixed atoms break the symmetry and make it polar.

Bond parameters & hydrogen bonding

Bond length — equilibrium distance between two bonded nuclei. Bond angle — angle between two bonds at a central atom. Bond enthalpy — energy to break one mole of bonds in the gaseous state: H2 435.8, O2 498, N2 946 kJ mol−1 (why N2 is so unreactive). Bond order — number of bonds between two atoms.

Hydrogen bond: the attractive force binding the hydrogen of one molecule to a highly electronegative atom — F, O or N. Strength 10–100 kJ mol−1.

The large electronegativity difference makes the H–F/O/N bond strongly polar, so H carries δ+ and is drawn to the lone pair on a δ− atom nearby.
  • Intermolecular — between different molecules: HF, H2O, NH3, alcohols. Raises boiling point.
  • Intramolecular — within one molecule: o-nitrophenol. Lowers boiling point, because no chain of molecules can form.

Consequences to quote: abnormally high boiling point of water vs H2S · ice is less dense than water (H-bonds hold an open cage structure) · high surface tension and viscosity · the structure of proteins and DNA.

Chapter 7  ·  Redox Reactions
What an oxidation number actually IS

Oxidation number is a bookkeeping device. It is the charge an atom would carry if every bond in the compound were 100% ionic — that is, if in every bond you handed both shared electrons entirely to the more electronegative atom.

It is not the real charge. In H2O oxygen is assigned −2, but its actual partial charge is only about −0.4 — the bond is polar covalent, not ionic. We pretend it is fully ionic so that electron movement can be tracked with whole numbers.

Why it was invented: in a covalent reaction nobody transfers a whole electron, so "loss of electrons" has no literal meaning. The oxidation number gives an unambiguous way to say whether an atom gained or lost control of electron density.

Where the rules come from: in H–Cl, Cl is more electronegative, so it takes both electrons → Cl = −1, H = +1. In Cl–Cl the two atoms are identical, so the pair is split evenly → each = 0. That is exactly why every free element is 0. Every rule below is just a consequence of relative electronegativities.

The seven rules, in priority order
  1. Free element = 0 — Na, O2, P4, S8, Cl2
  2. Monatomic ion = its charge — Na+ = +1, Cl− = −1
  3. F is always −1 (most electronegative element — it can never be positive)
  4. O is −2, except: −1 in peroxides (H2O2, Na2O2), −½ in superoxides (KO2), +2 in OF2
  5. H is +1, except −1 in metal hydrides (NaH, CaH2)
  6. Group 1 = +1, Group 2 = +2
  7. Sum = 0 for a neutral molecule; sum = the charge for an ion
Oxygen is −2, not +2. The only place oxygen is positive is OF2 (+2) and O2F2 (+1) — because fluorine is the one element more electronegative than oxygen.

Method: let the unknown be x, apply rule 7, solve.

KMnO4: (+1) + x + 4(−2) = 0 → Mn = +7
Cr2O72−: 2x + 7(−2) = −2 → Cr = +6
H2SO4: 2(+1) + x + 4(−2) = 0 → S = +6

OIL RIG — Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain.

Oxidising agent = accepts electrons = is itself reduced.
Reducing agent = donates electrons = is itself oxidised.

The agent does the opposite of what happens to it. An oxidising agent oxidises the other substance, so it itself gets reduced. This single confusion costs more marks than anything else in the chapter.
The four types of redox reaction

1 Combination — two species join, at least one an element

$\text{C}+\text{O}_2\to\text{CO}_2\qquad 3\text{Mg}+\text{N}_2\to\text{Mg}_3\text{N}_2$

2 Decomposition — one compound splits

$2\text{KClO}_3\to2\text{KCl}+3\text{O}_2\qquad 2\text{H}_2\text{O}\to2\text{H}_2+\text{O}_2$

3 Displacement — one ion or atom is replaced by another

$\text{Metal: }\ \text{Zn}+\text{CuSO}_4\to\text{ZnSO}_4+\text{Cu}$

$\text{Non-metal: }\ \text{Cl}_2+2\text{KBr}\to2\text{KCl}+\text{Br}_2;\quad 2\text{Na}+2\text{H}_2\text{O}\to2\text{NaOH}+\text{H}_2$

4 Disproportionation — the same element is simultaneously oxidised AND reduced

$2\text{H}_2\text{O}_2\to2\text{H}_2\text{O}+\text{O}_2\quad(\text{O}: -1\to-2\ \text{and}\ -1\to0)$

$\text{Cl}_2+2\text{OH}^-\to\text{Cl}^-+\text{ClO}^-+\text{H}_2\text{O}\quad(\text{Cl}: 0\to-1\ \text{and}\ 0\to+1)$

$\text{P}_4+3\text{OH}^-+3\text{H}_2\text{O}\to\text{PH}_3+3\text{H}_2\text{PO}_2^-$

Requirement for disproportionation: the element must have at least three oxidation states and must currently sit in an intermediate one — so it has room to move both up and down.
Ion–electron (half-reaction) method — nine steps
  1. Write the skeletal ionic equation
  2. Assign oxidation numbers; identify what is oxidised and what is reduced
  3. Split into two half-reactions
  4. Balance all atoms except O and H
  5. Balance O by adding H2O; balance H by adding H+
  6. Balance charge by adding electrons to the more positive side
  7. Multiply the half-reactions so the electrons are equal
  8. Add them; cancel the electrons
  9. Basic medium only: add as many OH− to BOTH sides as there are H+; combine H+ + OH− → H2O; cancel
Step 9 is the one people forget. If the question says alkaline / basic medium and your final answer still contains H+, you have not finished.
