Why do we need to classify elements?	Over 100 elements are known. Studying each individually is impossible, so they are grouped by <b>similar properties</b>, which lets the behaviour of a whole group be learned at once.
State Dobereiner's law of triads (1829) with examples.	In a group of three elements with similar properties, the <b>atomic weight of the middle element is approximately the mean</b> of the other two.<br>Examples: Li, Na, K &middot; Ca, Sr, Ba &middot; Cl, Br, I
Why did Dobereiner's triads fail?	Only a <b>very few triads</b> could be identified among the known elements, so it was not a general classification.
State Newlands' Law of Octaves (1865).	When elements are arranged in order of increasing atomic weight, <b>every eighth element has properties similar to the first</b>, like the eighth note of a musical scale.
Why did Newlands' Law of Octaves fail?	It was valid <b>only up to calcium</b>. Beyond that, elements with different properties fell into the same position, and there was no room for later-discovered elements.
Who proposed the Periodic Law in 1869, and independently of whom?	<b>Dmitri Mendeleev</b>, working independently of <b>Lothar Meyer</b>.
State Mendeleev's Periodic Law.	"The properties of the elements are a <b>periodic function of their atomic weights</b>."
How did Mendeleev arrange his table?	In <b>8 vertical groups</b> and <b>6 horizontal periods</b>, giving priority to <b>similarity of chemical properties</b> (especially formulae of hydrides and oxides) over strict atomic-weight order.
What was Mendeleev's most striking success?	He <b>left gaps</b> for undiscovered elements and <b>predicted their properties</b>: eka-aluminium (later <b>gallium</b>), eka-boron (<b>scandium</b>) and eka-silicon (<b>germanium</b>). All matched closely when found.
Give the anomalous atomic-weight pairs in Mendeleev's table.	He placed a heavier element before a lighter one to keep chemically similar elements together:<br><b>Ar (39.9) before K (39.1)</b>, <b>Co (58.9) before Ni (58.7)</b>, <b>Te (127.6) before I (126.9)</b>
What did Henry Moseley discover in 1913?	Plotting <b>&radic;&nu;</b> of the characteristic X-rays of an element against its <b>atomic number</b> gave a <b>straight line</b> &mdash; not so with atomic mass. So <b>atomic number</b>, not atomic weight, is the fundamental property.
State the Modern Periodic Law.	"The physical and chemical properties of the elements are <b>periodic functions of their atomic numbers</b>."
How many groups and periods are in the modern (long form) periodic table?	<b>18 vertical columns (groups)</b> and <b>7 horizontal rows (periods)</b>.
Why does periodicity occur at all?	Because of the <b>recurrence of similar valence shell electronic configurations</b> after regular intervals as the atomic number increases.
Give the number of elements in each of the seven periods.	Period 1: <b>2</b><br>Period 2: <b>8</b><br>Period 3: <b>8</b><br>Period 4: <b>18</b><br>Period 5: <b>18</b><br>Period 6: <b>32</b><br>Period 7: <b>32</b>
Which subshells are filled in each period?	1: 1s<br>2: 2s 2p<br>3: 3s 3p<br>4: 4s 3d 4p<br>5: 5s 4d 5p<br>6: 6s 4f 5d 6p<br>7: 7s 5f 6d 7p
What determines the period number of an element?	The <b>principal quantum number (n) of its valence shell</b> &mdash; the highest n in its configuration.
Why are lanthanoids and actinoids placed separately below the table?	To keep the table <b>18 columns wide</b>. Placing all 14 f-block elements in periods 6 and 7 would make the table unmanageably long.
Why is a special nomenclature needed for elements with Z > 100?	Because such elements are made in tiny amounts and their discovery was often disputed, IUPAC gives a <b>temporary systematic name</b> built from the digits of the atomic number until an official name is agreed.
Give the IUPAC numerical roots 0 to 9.	0 <b>nil</b>, 1 <b>un</b>, 2 <b>bi</b>, 3 <b>tri</b>, 4 <b>quad</b>, 5 <b>pent</b>, 6 <b>hex</b>, 7 <b>sept</b>, 8 <b>oct</b>, 9 <b>enn</b>
How is the IUPAC systematic name and symbol formed for Z > 100?	Write the roots for each digit in order, add the suffix <b>-ium</b>. The symbol is the <b>first letter of each root</b>, giving three letters.
Give the IUPAC systematic name and symbol for element 104 and element 120.	104 = un + nil + quad + ium = <b>unnilquadium (Unq)</b>, now rutherfordium (Rf)<br>120 = un + bi + nil + ium = <b>unbinilium (Ubn)</b>
What spelling adjustments are made in IUPAC systematic names?	The final <b>n</b> of "enn" is dropped before "nil", and the final <b>i</b> of "bi" and "tri" is dropped before "ium".
Name the four blocks of the periodic table and the groups they occupy.	<b>s-block</b>: groups 1 and 2<br><b>p-block</b>: groups 13 to 18<br><b>d-block</b>: groups 3 to 12<br><b>f-block</b>: lanthanoids and actinoids, placed separately
General outer electronic configuration of the s-block?	<b>ns<sup>1-2</sup></b>
Give the characteristics of s-block elements.	Group 1 = <b>alkali metals</b>, group 2 = <b>alkaline earth metals</b>. They have <b>low ionization enthalpies</b>, readily <b>lose</b> their outer electrons to give +1 or +2 ions, are <b>very reactive metals</b> and <b>good reducing agents</b>. Metallic character increases down the group.
General outer electronic configuration of the p-block?	<b>ns<sup>2</sup>np<sup>1-6</sup></b>
What are representative (main group) elements?	The <b>s-block and p-block</b> elements taken together (groups 1, 2 and 13&ndash;18).
Describe group 18 elements.	The <b>noble gases</b>, with <b>ns<sup>2</sup>np<sup>6</sup></b> closed-shell configurations (helium is 1s<sup>2</sup>). They are chemically <b>very unreactive</b>.
What are groups 17 and 16 called, and what is characteristic of them?	Group 17 = <b>halogens</b>, group 16 = <b>chalcogens</b>. Both have <b>highly negative electron gain enthalpies</b> and readily accept electrons to reach noble gas configuration.
General outer electronic configuration of the d-block?	<b>(n&minus;1)d<sup>1-10</sup> ns<sup>0-2</sup></b>
Name the four transition series of the d-block.	<b>3d</b> (Sc &rarr; Zn), <b>4d</b> (Y &rarr; Cd), <b>5d</b> (La, Hf &rarr; Hg), <b>6d</b> (Ac, Rf onward)
Give the characteristic properties of transition elements.	All are <b>metals</b>; they form <b>coloured ions</b>, show <b>variable oxidation states</b>, are <b>paramagnetic</b>, and are widely used as <b>catalysts</b>.
Why are Zn, Cd and Hg not regarded as transition elements?	Their configuration is <b>(n&minus;1)d<sup>10</sup>ns<sup>2</sup></b> &mdash; the d subshell is <b>completely filled in both the atom and its common ions</b>, so they do not show typical transition-metal behaviour.
General outer electronic configuration of the f-block, and what are they called?	<b>(n&minus;2)f<sup>1-14</sup> (n&minus;1)d<sup>0-1</sup> ns<sup>2</sup></b>. They are the <b>inner transition elements</b>.
Name the two f-block series with their atomic number ranges.	<b>Lanthanoids</b>: Ce(58) to Lu(71), following lanthanum<br><b>Actinoids</b>: Th(90) to Lr(103), following actinium
Give the characteristics of the f-block elements.	All are <b>metals</b>, showing mainly the <b>+3</b> oxidation state. The <b>actinoids are radioactive</b>. Elements after uranium are called <b>transuranium elements</b>.
Roughly what fraction of the elements are metals, and where are they placed?	<b>About 78%</b> of all known elements are metals. They occupy the <b>left side and centre</b> of the periodic table.
Where are non-metals located, and how do their numbers compare to metals?	On the <b>upper right-hand side</b> of the table. There are <b>far fewer</b> non-metals than metals.
What are metalloids, and name them.	Elements with properties <b>intermediate between metals and non-metals</b>, lying along the borderline: <b>boron, silicon, germanium, arsenic, antimony and tellurium</b>.
How does metallic and non-metallic character vary across a period and down a group?	<b>Across a period (left to right)</b>: metallic character <b>decreases</b>, non-metallic character <b>increases</b>.<br><b>Down a group</b>: metallic character <b>increases</b>, non-metallic character <b>decreases</b>.
Why is atomic radius hard to define?	An atom has <b>no sharp boundary</b> &mdash; the electron cloud has no definite edge &mdash; and an isolated atom cannot be isolated for measurement. So radius is always defined <b>operationally</b>, from bonded atoms.
Define covalent radius with an example.	<b>Half the internuclear distance</b> between two identical atoms joined by a <b>single covalent bond</b>. In Cl<sub>2</sub> the nuclei are 198 pm apart, so the covalent radius of Cl is <b>99 pm</b>.
Define van der Waals radius and say where it is used.	<b>Half the distance between the nuclei of two adjacent non-bonded atoms</b> of neighbouring molecules in the solid state. Used for the <b>noble gases</b>, which do not form covalent bonds.
Define metallic radius.	<b>Half the internuclear distance between two adjacent metal atoms</b> in a metallic crystal.
How does atomic radius change across a period, and why?	It <b>decreases</b>. The nuclear charge increases while electrons are added to the <b>same shell</b>, so the increased nuclear attraction pulls the electron cloud inward.
How does atomic radius change down a group, and why?	It <b>increases</b>, because a <b>new principal shell is added</b> at each step and the inner electrons shield the outer ones from the nucleus.
Give the trend in atomic radii across period 2 (Li to F).	Li <b>152</b>, Be <b>111</b>, B <b>88</b>, C <b>77</b>, N <b>74</b>, O <b>66</b>, F <b>64</b> pm &mdash; a steady decrease.
Give the trend in atomic radii down group 1.	Li <b>152</b>, Na <b>186</b>, K <b>231</b>, Rb <b>244</b>, Cs <b>262</b> pm &mdash; a steady increase.
Why is a cation smaller than its parent atom?	It has <b>fewer electrons</b> while the <b>nuclear charge is unchanged</b>, so the remaining electrons feel a greater effective nuclear charge and are pulled in more tightly.
Why is an anion larger than its parent atom?	<b>Extra electrons</b> are added with <b>no increase in nuclear charge</b>, so electron&ndash;electron repulsion increases and the effective nuclear charge per electron falls, expanding the cloud.
Define isoelectronic species.	Atoms and ions having the <b>same number of electrons</b> but <b>different nuclear charges</b>. Example: O<sup>2&minus;</sup>, F<sup>&minus;</sup>, Na<sup>+</sup>, Mg<sup>2+</sup>, Al<sup>3+</sup> &mdash; all with 10 electrons.
How does radius vary within an isoelectronic series?	The <b>greater the positive charge (higher nuclear charge), the smaller the radius</b>.<br>O<sup>2&minus;</sup> 140 &gt; F<sup>&minus;</sup> 136 &gt; Na<sup>+</sup> 95 &gt; Mg<sup>2+</sup> 65 &gt; Al<sup>3+</sup> 50 pm
Define ionization enthalpy and give the equation.	The <b>energy required to remove an electron from an isolated gaseous atom in its ground state</b>.<br>X(g) &rarr; X<sup>+</sup>(g) + e<sup>&minus;</sup><br>It is always <b>endothermic (positive)</b>, measured in kJ mol<sup>&minus;1</sup>.
Why is the second ionization enthalpy always greater than the first?	After the first electron is removed the species is <b>positively charged</b>, so the remaining electrons are held <b>more tightly</b> by the same nuclear charge. Hence &Delta;<sub>i</sub>H<sub>1</sub> &lt; &Delta;<sub>i</sub>H<sub>2</sub> &lt; &Delta;<sub>i</sub>H<sub>3</sub>.
On what factors does ionization enthalpy depend?	<b>Atomic size</b> (larger &rarr; lower IE), <b>nuclear charge</b> (larger &rarr; higher IE), the <b>shielding/screening effect</b> of inner electrons, and the <b>penetration</b> of the outer subshell (s &gt; p &gt; d &gt; f).
How does ionization enthalpy vary across a period and down a group?	<b>Across a period</b>: increases, because size decreases and nuclear charge increases.<br><b>Down a group</b>: decreases, because size increases and shielding increases.
Which element in a period has the highest ionization enthalpy, and which the lowest?	Highest: the <b>noble gas</b> (stable closed shell). Lowest: the <b>alkali metal</b>.
Give the first ionization enthalpies across period 2.	Li 520, Be 899, B 801, C 1086, N 1402, O 1314, F 1681, Ne 2080 kJ mol<sup>&minus;1</sup><br>Note the two dips: <b>Be &gt; B</b> and <b>N &gt; O</b>.
Why is the ionization enthalpy of boron LESS than that of beryllium?	Be is 1s<sup>2</sup>2s<sup>2</sup>; B is 1s<sup>2</sup>2s<sup>2</sup>2p<sup>1</sup>. Boron's outgoing electron comes from a <b>2p orbital, which is higher in energy and better shielded</b> by the 2s electrons, so <b>less energy is needed</b> to remove it than to break beryllium's filled 2s<sup>2</sup>.
Why is the ionization enthalpy of oxygen LESS than that of nitrogen?	N is 2p<sup>3</sup> &mdash; an <b>exactly half-filled, symmetrical and extra-stable</b> configuration. O is 2p<sup>4</sup>, where one 2p orbital holds a <b>pair</b>; the <b>inter-electronic repulsion</b> in that pair helps push an electron out, so less energy is required.
Define electron gain enthalpy and give the equation.	The <b>enthalpy change when an electron is added to a neutral gaseous atom</b> to give an anion.<br>X(g) + e<sup>&minus;</sup> &rarr; X<sup>&minus;</sup>(g)<br><b>Negative</b> if energy is released, <b>positive</b> if energy must be supplied.
Which group has the most negative electron gain enthalpies, and why?	The <b>halogens (group 17)</b>. They are one electron short of a stable noble gas configuration, so they accept an electron very readily.
Why do noble gases have large positive electron gain enthalpies?	Their shells are completely filled, so an incoming electron would have to enter the <b>next higher energy level</b> &mdash; energy must be supplied.
How does electron gain enthalpy vary across a period and down a group?	<b>Across a period</b>: becomes <b>more negative</b>.<br><b>Down a group</b>: becomes <b>less negative</b>, as atomic size increases.
Why is the electron gain enthalpy of O and F LESS negative than that of S and Cl?	Because O and F are <b>very small</b>, so their <b>2p subshell is compact</b> and already crowded. The incoming electron suffers strong <b>inter-electronic repulsion</b>. The larger 3p subshells of S and Cl accommodate it much more easily.
Give the electron gain enthalpy values for the halogens and for O and S.	F <b>&minus;328</b>, Cl <b>&minus;349</b>, Br <b>&minus;325</b>, I <b>&minus;295</b><br>O <b>&minus;141</b>, S <b>&minus;200</b> kJ mol<sup>&minus;1</sup>
Why is the second electron gain enthalpy always positive?	A second electron must be forced onto an <b>already negative ion</b>, which repels it, so energy has to be supplied. Example: O<sup>&minus;</sup>(g) + e<sup>&minus;</sup> &rarr; O<sup>2&minus;</sup>(g)
Define electronegativity.	The <b>ability of an atom in a chemical compound to attract shared electrons</b> towards itself. It cannot be measured directly and is only a <b>relative</b> quantity.
Which is the most electronegative element, and what is its Pauling value?	<b>Fluorine</b>, with a Pauling electronegativity of <b>4.0</b> &mdash; the highest of all elements.
How does electronegativity vary across a period and down a group?	<b>Across a period</b>: increases (size decreases, nuclear charge increases).<br><b>Down a group</b>: decreases (size increases).
How does electronegativity relate to metallic and non-metallic character?	<b>Non-metallic character increases</b> with increasing electronegativity; <b>metallic character increases</b> with decreasing electronegativity. So electronegativity is a good measure of non-metallic character.
Distinguish electron gain enthalpy from electronegativity.	<b>Electron gain enthalpy</b> refers to an <b>isolated gaseous atom</b> and is a measurable energy in kJ mol<sup>&minus;1</sup>.<br><b>Electronegativity</b> refers to an atom <b>within a compound</b> and is a relative, unitless number.
How is valence related to the group number for representative elements?	Valence = <b>number of valence electrons</b>, or <b>8 minus that number</b>, whichever is smaller/appropriate.
Give the formulae of the oxides across period 3.	Na<sub>2</sub>O, MgO, Al<sub>2</sub>O<sub>3</sub>, SiO<sub>2</sub>, P<sub>4</sub>O<sub>10</sub>, SO<sub>3</sub>, Cl<sub>2</sub>O<sub>7</sub> &mdash; highest oxidation state rises <b>1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7</b>.
Give the formulae of the hydrides across period 3.	NaH, MgH<sub>2</sub>, AlH<sub>3</sub>, SiH<sub>4</sub>, PH<sub>3</sub>, H<sub>2</sub>S, HCl
State the three reasons for the anomalous behaviour of second period elements.	1. <b>Small size</b><br>2. <b>Large charge/radius ratio</b> and <b>high electronegativity</b><br>3. <b>Absence of d orbitals</b> in the valence shell
What is the maximum covalency of second period elements, and how does it compare with later periods?	Second period elements have a maximum covalency of <b>4</b> (e.g. BF<sub>4</sub><sup>&minus;</sup>), because they have no d orbitals. Later periods can exceed this &mdash; aluminium forms <b>AlF<sub>6</sub><sup>3&minus;</sup></b> using d orbitals.
What is the diagonal relationship? Give the three pairs.	The first element of a group resembles the <b>second element of the next group</b>, placed diagonally.<br><b>Li &ndash; Mg</b>, <b>Be &ndash; Al</b>, <b>B &ndash; Si</b>
Where in a period is chemical reactivity highest, and why?	At the <b>two extremes</b>. On the left, metals have <b>low ionization enthalpy</b> and lose electrons easily; on the right, non-metals have <b>highly negative electron gain enthalpy</b> and gain electrons easily. Elements in the <b>centre</b> are the least reactive.
Which group is the least reactive, and why?	<b>Group 18, the noble gases</b> &mdash; they have completely filled valence shells, so they neither lose nor gain electrons readily.
How does the nature of oxides change across a period?	From <b>basic</b> on the left (Na<sub>2</sub>O, MgO), through <b>amphoteric</b> (Al<sub>2</sub>O<sub>3</sub>), to <b>acidic</b> on the right (SO<sub>3</sub>, Cl<sub>2</sub>O<sub>7</sub>).
What is an amphoteric oxide? Give an example with equations.	One that reacts with <b>both acids and bases</b>.<br>Al<sub>2</sub>O<sub>3</sub> + 6HCl &rarr; 2AlCl<sub>3</sub> + 3H<sub>2</sub>O<br>Al<sub>2</sub>O<sub>3</sub> + 2NaOH &rarr; 2NaAlO<sub>2</sub> + H<sub>2</sub>O
Give examples of neutral oxides.	<b>CO, NO and N<sub>2</sub>O</b> &mdash; they are neither acidic nor basic.
How does the reducing power of metals vary down a group and across a period?	<b>Down a group</b>: increases, as ionization enthalpy falls.<br><b>Across a period</b>: decreases, as ionization enthalpy rises and the elements become more non-metallic.
